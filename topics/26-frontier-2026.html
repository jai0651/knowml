2026 Frontier Map

Follow one idea through the whole field: instead of a bigger model, spend compute per question and build systems around a fixed model. You'll see how that works, where it's been applied, what it costs, and which problems it leaves open.

Imagine you ask a language model two questions in a row: what's the capital of France, and can it prove a theorem. Everything the model knows came from pretraining, the long and expensive run where it learns from a huge pile of text, and pretraining spent exactly the same effort preparing for both questions. By the time you ask, that effort has been spent. Nothing in the model can say that the second question deserves more thought than the first. For years, the only way to get a better answer to the hard question was to build a bigger model, which meant paying more up front for every question, easy ones included.

By 2025 and 2026 that recipe was getting expensive faster than it was getting useful, and a lot of the field's energy moved to what you do around a fixed model. Most directly, you spend extra compute at inference time, meaning while the model is answering, and to spend it only on the hard questions. You sample several candidate answers, score them with a checker called a verifier, and keep the best one. On the right kind of problem, that can beat a much larger model for less total cost.

That shift shows up one level higher as agentic systems. Instead of asking one model to do everything in a single pass, you wrap it in a loop that plans, calls tools, checks its own work and tries again, so a monolithic black box becomes an inspectable pipeline of pieces that are each individually reliable. Multimodality, robotics and science followed a similar arc: bolt a pretrained backbone onto a new kind of input or body instead of starting from scratch.

This chapter is a map more than a single argument. We'll start with a small experiment that shows why spending compute per question works at all. Then we'll walk through the five directions, look at the two mechanisms that recur underneath most of them, and see what each one costs. Last, we'll look at the problems that all of this left open: reliability at the tail, grounding claims in evidence, planning over long horizons, and evaluation itself. If you need one sentence for an interview, the 2026 frontier is less "a bigger model" and more "a system built around a fixed model," and the honest caveat is that most of the open problems from 2023 are still open.

01Spending compute when you know the question

Let's start with what a trained model is. It carries a fixed amount of knowledge and skill, put there by pretraining and by fine-tuning, which is further training on narrower examples. Call that its raw competence. Historically, the way to get more of it was to make the model bigger, train it on more data, or both, and all of that happens before it has answered a single question.

That runs into a ceiling. Pretraining compute is a sunk, fixed cost, and the model can't tell in advance which questions deserve more effort, because by the time it's answering, the training compute is already gone. So think about flipping the order. Inference-time compute is the compute you spend while the model answers, and the idea is to spend a little more per question, adaptively, at the moment you know what the question is, and less up front. You can sample several attempts, search over partial solutions, or run a verifier to catch mistakes. An easy question gets one quick pass, and a hard one gets ten attempts and a checker.

How much can repeated attempts buy?

Here's the cheapest version of the idea, and it needs no verifier at all. Ask the model the same question many times and take the majority answer. To see how far that can go, suppose each attempt is right 60% of the time and the attempts are independent of each other, so one attempt being wrong tells you nothing about the next.

Below, the code runs that as a simulation. rng.random((100_000, k)) < p builds a grid with 100,000 rows, one per simulated question, and k columns, one per attempt, and each cell is True with probability 0.6. Summing a row counts the correct attempts, and the row's majority is right when more than half of its attempts are correct.

Try it Take a majority vote over repeated attempts 
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
p = 0.6                                   # chance one attempt is right

for k in (1, 5, 15, 51):
    votes = rng.random((100_000, k)) < p  # k independent attempts
    majority = votes.sum(axis=1) > k / 2
    print(f"{k:>2} attempts, majority vote: right {majority.mean():.1%}")
One attempt is right 60% of the time. The majority of 5 is right about 68% of the time, of 15 about 79%, and of 51 about 93%. The gain holds only if the mistakes are independent. Attempts from the same model can fail in the same way, so real gains are smaller than this idealised case.

Look at how the accuracy moves. Going from one attempt to five buys eight points, and going from fifteen to fifty-one buys fourteen more, but at more than three times the number of attempts. Each extra vote helps a little less than the one before. That caveat in the output matters too. We drew every attempt independently in the simulation, but attempts from the same model often fail in the same way, because they share the same blind spot, so the real curve sits below this one.

Majority voting only works when answers can be compared directly, like a final number. Most of the frontier is about doing better than counting votes, by scoring candidates instead, and by applying the same spend-where-needed idea in other places. Agentic systems apply it one level up. Instead of forcing a single model call to plan, execute and self-correct all at once, you split those into separate steps that a system runs in a loop, stopping early on easy tasks and iterating longer on hard ones. Multimodal and embodied work is a different kind of reuse: take a capable pretrained backbone and point it at a new input or output, like video or robot actions, instead of learning that from zero. Broad world knowledge and reasoning is the expensive part, and it transfers, while only the comparatively cheap part, how to perceive or act in the new setting, has to be learned fresh. Before we go through those directions one at a time, a short look at how they arrived together.

02Where this came from

Two lines of work met around 2022 to 2024. Chain-of-thought prompting (2022) showed that asking a model to think step by step improved its accuracy on multi-step problems with no change to the architecture, and that raised an obvious question: if the model's written-out scratch work helps, why not spend more compute on that scratch work and pick the best result? That became test-time-compute scaling and process-supervised verifiers (2023–2024), which section 3 describes. In parallel, RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback, a tuning step that pushes a model toward answers people prefer) and instruction tuning, both covered in chapter 10, made models reliable enough at following instructions and calling tools that you could wire them into agentic loops that act, observe a result and re-plan. Chapter 11 covers the retrieval and single-step tool-use side of that. What follows takes five directions the field pushed on, each a response to a specific limitation of what came before, and then the problems none of them fixed.

03The landscape, in five directions

All five directions make the same kind of move: reuse something that already works, or spend extra only where it's needed. We'll go from the one closest to our voting experiment to the one furthest from it, using a small concrete case for each, a hard math problem, a bug report, a video, a robot arm and a protein.

Reasoning & inference-time compute

Take a hard math problem. Voting works if the answer is a single number, but we'd like to do better than counting. Snell et al. (see Go Deeper) formalize two ways to spend compute on one question. One is search against a verifier: sample N candidate solutions, score each with a model trained to judge correctness, and keep the best. The other is adaptive revision, where the model refines its own draft over several rounds, each conditioned on the prompt and the draft so far.

What's a verifier made of? Usually it's a process reward model (PRM), which scores each intermediate reasoning step as well as the final answer. Lightman et al.'s "Let's Verify Step by Step" showed why that's worth the trouble. Process supervision caught errors that outcome supervision let through, because a right final answer can hide a wrong method that happened to land correctly. Scoring only the final number would reward that lucky solution, and scoring the steps wouldn't. That leaves a question about what happens when the work isn't one answer but a long sequence of actions in the world, which is where agents come in.

Agentic systems

Chapter 11 already covers the core loop of reasoning, a tool call, an observation, and repeat. That loop is called ReAct. What changes here is the long horizon. Picture a coding agent handed a bug report. It plans a multi-file change, writes the code, runs the test suite, reads the failure and retries, possibly dozens of times.

That asks for three things a single-step loop doesn't need. It needs memory, to carry state from one step to the next. It needs planning, to break the goal into sub-goals. And it needs self-verification: deciding for itself when a sub-goal succeeded, and that takes more than running without crashing. That last one is the verifier idea from the previous direction, applied to each step of a plan.

How does an agent know when to reach for a tool in the first place? Toolformer (Schick et al., see Go Deeper) was an early demonstration that a model can learn when to call a tool, and with what arguments, from self-supervised examples instead of hand-scripted rules. It underlies how modern agent frameworks decide to reach for a calculator, a search engine or a code interpreter.

The multimodal & efficiency frontier

Next come two threads that both push a capable backbone into new territory. The first is multimodal. Chapter 14 covers the bolt-on "encoder + projector + LLM" recipe versus training natively multimodal from the start. The frontier pushes native multimodality across more modalities at once, so text, image, audio and video live in one model, and toward generative video that stays temporally consistent enough to work as an interactive world simulator instead of a short clip generator. That's a direct extension of the diffusion techniques in chapter 12, applied across time as well as space.

Efficiency is the second thread, and it comes from the other side. Mixture-of-Experts (Switch Transformers, see Go Deeper, covered in depth in chapter 10) decouples a model's total parameter count from the compute it spends per token, so capacity can grow without inference cost growing at the same rate. Section 4 shows how. Distillation, where a small model is trained to reproduce a large model's behavior, and quantization, where weights are stored with fewer bits (both in chapter 23), push the other way: they take a large model's abilities and compress them small enough to run on a device. Both threads chase one goal: more capability per dollar of inference compute and less reliance on training compute.

The embodied & robotics frontier

Now put the backbone in a robot arm. Vision-language-action models (chapter 21) and world models used as learned simulators for planning (chapter 22) are the same "reuse a pretrained backbone" idea, applied to physical action instead of text or code. You start from a model that already understands images and language and fine-tune it to output robot actions as an additional kind of token. That beats training a control policy from scratch on a small amount of robot data.

Cross-embodiment generalization (chapter 21) pushes further. It pools data across many different robot bodies so that a single policy transfers between them. It's the same logic that makes a pretrained language model reusable across many downstream text tasks.

AI for science

Last, a protein. Here the evidence is that a general architecture, plus the right training objective and enough of the right data, carries over well beyond language and images. AlphaFold2 (Jumper et al., see Go Deeper) treated protein structure prediction as a geometric learning problem and reached accuracy that decades of physics-based simulation had not. It became Nature's and Science's Method of the Year for 2021 and, per the community assessments cited in follow-up literature, changed how structural biologists work day to day.

That same recipe of a domain-appropriate architecture, a large corpus of structured natural data and a well-chosen objective has since been extended toward molecule and materials design. Those domains are individually less mature than protein structure prediction, and they remain active, unsettled research areas. Across all five directions, two mechanisms recur often enough to be worth stating exactly.

04The core mechanisms, precisely

This chapter spans too many sub-fields to share one equation, the way a single-architecture chapter does. But best-of-N selection and sparse routing show up under so many of the directions above that we'll take them apart here.

Best-of-N with a verifier

Back to our hard math problem. Given the prompt, sample N candidate completions from the model, independently and usually at temperature above zero, a setting that makes the model's sampling random enough for the candidates to differ. Score each one with a verifier V and return argmax_i V(candidate_i), the candidate with the highest score. The verifier can be a separate trained model, such as a PRM scoring each reasoning step. In simpler setups it's a rule-based checker that asks whether the code compiles or whether the final answer matches a known constraint.

What does this cost? Larger N costs inference compute linearly, while the accuracy gains are sublinear and eventually flatten, the same shape our voting simulation showed. That sets up the compute-optimal allocation problem Snell et al. study: for a fixed budget, is it better to spend on more samples, or on letting one sample think longer?

Sparse routing (Mixture-of-Experts)

Our second mechanism attacks the cost from the other side. A router network computes, for each token, a score over a bank of expert feed-forward networks. Only the top-k highest-scoring experts, commonly k=1 or k=2, process that token. All the others contribute nothing to that token's forward pass, and cost nothing for it.

That's what lets total parameter count scale with the number of experts while compute per token stays pinned to the work of k experts. It's also why sparse models can hold far more total parameters than a dense model trained at the same per-token compute budget. Chapter 10 covers the training-time complications this introduces, like balancing the load across experts. With both mechanisms in hand, we can ask why the field went this way at all.

05Why this direction

Why not just keep scaling pretraining?

It was the obvious path, and it worked. More parameters and more data reliably paid off from GPT-2 through GPT-4-class models. The trouble is the same one from the start of the chapter: a fixed pretraining budget is spent identically on easy and hard questions. Spending compute adaptively at inference time, per query, puts it only where it's needed.

That has a price. Per-query latency and serving cost become higher and less predictable, since a hard query might take 10× longer, or run 10× more samples, than an easy one. That complicates capacity planning compared with a model whose inference cost is fixed.

Snell et al.'s headline result puts a number on what the price buys. Under a matched compute budget, their compute-optimal test-time strategy let a smaller model outperform a roughly 14× larger model, on problems where the smaller model already had some non-trivial baseline success rate. Here's the part to say out loud in an interview: that result depended on the problem having a verifiable or scoreable structure, like math or code, where a verifier or search strategy can tell a better answer from a worse one. For open-ended tasks with no clean way to score a candidate, spending more inference-time compute has much weaker guarantees. That's why verifier design and evaluation (chapter 25) are as much a frontier problem as raw compute allocation.

Why break an agent's work into tool calls?

A monolithic model has to learn arithmetic, retrieval and code execution as internal skills, imperfectly, from training data. Routing each of them to a real calculator, search index or code interpreter does that sub-task exactly. A wrong tool call is also visible and debuggable, in a way a wrong computation buried inside a forward pass isn't. But every one of these gains comes with its own way of failing, and we should name them.

06Complexity, failure modes, and the persistent open problems

Inference-time search inherits a classic optimization failure called reward hacking against the verifier. Every verifier is imperfect to some degree. If you search aggressively, you can find a candidate that scores well under the verifier without being correct, the same way a reinforcement-learning policy can learn to exploit a misspecified reward function (chapter 15). Perversely, the more compute you spend searching, the more thoroughly you hunt for exactly this kind of exploit, whether you meant to or not.

Agentic loops fail differently, through compounding error over long horizons. Say an agent's per-step success rate is 95% (an illustrative number, not a benchmark result) and a task takes 50 sequential steps. If errors are independent, the probability that every step succeeds is roughly 0.95^50 ≈ 7.7%. So the bottleneck for autonomous coding is long-horizon reliability, more than per-step accuracy. It also shows why self-verification at intermediate steps matters far more here than in a single-shot call: it catches an error at step 12 before it surfaces at step 50.

Beyond those two, there's the honest list of problems that carried over unchanged from 2023 and that none of the directions above solved. Reliability is the first. Models are still confidently wrong at a non-trivial rate, and inference-time compute reduces that without eliminating it. Grounding is closely related: tying a claim to verifiable evidence and not to a fluent-sounding guess (see hallucination and groundedness in chapter 25). Long-horizon planning is the compounding-error problem we just computed, unresolved for long, ambiguous real-world tasks.

Evaluation gets worse as models improve. Benchmark contamination and the weaknesses of LLM-as-judge (chapter 25) both grow as models get better at sounding right. Cost is a direct trade, because inference-time compute exchanges money and latency for accuracy and there's no free lunch in that. Finally, safety and controllability: an agent that can execute code and call tools has a much larger blast radius when it does the wrong thing than a model that only outputs text.

None of this is a reason for pessimism about the field. It's the current research agenda, and naming these problems specifically gives a more informed answer than gesturing vaguely at "AI safety."

07Summary

  1. Pretraining spends the same compute on every question, easy or hard, and has already spent it by the time the model answers. Gains from making it bigger got expensive faster than they got useful.
  2. Inference-time compute is spent per question. Sampling several attempts, searching over partial solutions or running a verifier gives easy questions one pass and hard ones many.
  3. Voting helps, with diminishing returns. At 60% per attempt, the majority of 5 is right about 68% of the time and of 51 about 93%, but only if mistakes are independent, and attempts from one model often aren't.
  4. A verifier does better than a vote. Best-of-N scores each candidate and keeps the best, and a process reward model scores each reasoning step and catches right answers reached by wrong methods.
  5. Agentic systems wrap a model in a loop of planning, tool calls and self-checking, and it needs memory, planning and self-verification once the horizon gets long.
  6. Backbones get reused. Native multimodality, vision-language-action models and AlphaFold-style science all start from something that already works and learn only the new part.
  7. Mixture-of-Experts decouples parameters from per-token compute, since only the top-k experts run for a token. Distillation and quantization compress the other way.
  8. The gains depend on being able to score an answer. Snell et al.'s result, a smaller model beating one roughly 14× larger, holds where a verifier can tell better from worse, like math and code.
  9. Search can hack its own verifier. The harder you search, the more likely you are to find a candidate that scores well without being correct.
  10. Long agent runs compound error. A 95% per-step success rate over 50 steps leaves about 7.7% end to end, so intermediate self-verification matters.
  11. The old problems are still open: reliability, grounding, long-horizon planning, evaluation, cost, and safety and controllability.

08Build this

Every claim on this page rests on one curve: accuracy against sampling budget. You can measure it yourself in an afternoon, and the shape it comes out is more informative than any summary of it.

Project Measure the gap between finding an answer and picking it ~3 hours · any chat API or local model

Take a set of problems with automatically checkable answers, grade-school math being the standard choice. Sample N answers per problem at non-zero temperature. Then plot two curves on the same axes as N grows: how often any of the N samples is correct, and how often your selection rule picks a correct one.

  1. Pick 100 problems where the answer is a number, so grading is exact string comparison and never a judgement call.
  2. Sample N=16 answers per problem at temperature around 0.8. Cache every sample to disk. This is the only slow step, and you pay for it once.
  3. From that cache, compute both curves for N = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 by subsampling, with no new API calls.
  4. Curve one is the oracle: was any sample right. Curve two is majority vote: take the most common final answer.
  5. Now change the selection rule. Score each candidate by its own average token log-probability and pick the highest, then plot that as a third curve.
You'll know it worked when the two curves separate and keep separating as N grows. The oracle curve rises; majority vote rises more slowly and starts to flatten. That widening gap is the entire test-time-compute argument in one picture: the model already produced a correct answer, and the thing standing between you and it is selection.
What the third curve teaches. Self-reported confidence is a weak selector, and on some problem sets it does worse than plain majority vote. A model can be fluent and wrong with high probability on every token. That is the argument for training a separate verifier on reasoning steps rather than reading confidence off the generator, and it is why process reward models exist at all.

Where this runs in production

An autonomous coding agent, given a bug report, runs a loop:

  • Plan. Identify likely files, form a hypothesis.
  • Act. It writes a code change.
  • Verify. Run the existing test suite.
  • Revise. If tests fail, read the failure output and try again. Loop until tests pass or a step or time budget is exhausted.

This is the agentic loop from section 03, with the test suite standing in as an automatic, domain-specific verifier. It has the same "generate, verify, keep or retry" shape as best-of-N sampling in section 04, spread across sequential steps instead of parallel samples. And it inherits the compounding-error risk from section 06 whenever a multi-file change needs many sequential correct steps.

09Interview questions

ConceptWhy might a smaller model with more inference-time compute beat a larger model with less?

Because pretraining compute is spent identically on every future query regardless of difficulty, while inference-time compute (sampling, search, verification) can be allocated adaptively, per query, only where it's needed — an easy query doesn't need the extra spend, a hard one benefits from it directly. Snell et al. show this concretely: under a matched total compute budget, adaptive test-time strategies let a smaller model outperform a much larger one on problems with verifiable structure. The caveat worth stating: this depends on having a way to actually score candidate answers (math, code) — it's a much weaker effect on open-ended tasks without a clean verifier.

System designWhy does an agent's reliability degrade so much faster than its per-step accuracy would suggest?

Because end-to-end success requires every step in a long sequential chain to succeed, and independent per-step error compounds multiplicatively — a 95% (illustrative) per-step success rate over 50 sequential steps gives roughly 0.95^50 ≈ 7.7% end-to-end success. The fix isn't a better single model call, it's catching errors at intermediate steps (self-verification) so a mistake at step 12 gets corrected before it propagates to step 50, rather than being discovered only at the end.

Failure modesWhat's "reward hacking against a verifier," and why does more search make it worse, not better?

A verifier is itself an imperfect, learned approximation of "is this actually correct." Aggressive search or sampling explores a huge space of candidates specifically looking for whatever scores highest under that verifier — the more thoroughly you search, the more likely you are to find an edge case where the verifier's score and true correctness diverge, which is a structurally identical failure to reward hacking in RL (15): optimizing hard against an imperfect proxy objective finds exactly the proxy's blind spots.

Honesty checkName two problems from 2023 that are still unsolved in 2026, and say why.

Reliability and evaluation are two honest answers. Reliability: inference-time compute and verifiers reduce error rates but don't eliminate confidently-wrong outputs, because the verifier itself is an imperfect model, not ground truth. Evaluation: LLM-as-judge and benchmark-based evaluation (25) get harder, not easier, as models improve, because a more fluent model is also more capable of sounding correct while being wrong, and benchmarks leak into training data over time (contamination). Naming the actual mechanism behind why a problem is still open reads as far more credible than just listing "safety" or "hallucination" as buzzwords.
