Modern Vision Foundation Models

How attention took over vision: patches-as-tokens, contrastive image-text pretraining, promptable segmentation, and the encoder-projector-LLM recipe that turns all of it into a vision-language model.

Know cold ~24 min · prerequisites: convolutional inductive bias (05), the transformer block (08)
TL;DR

A Vision Transformer (ViT) chops an image into fixed-size patches, treats each patch as a token, and runs the exact same transformer encoder from page 08 over the resulting sequence — no convolutions, no built-in notion of "nearby pixels matter more," and it still works, given enough data. CLIP trains an image encoder and a text encoder together so that matching image-caption pairs land close together in a shared embedding space and mismatched pairs land far apart — which is what makes zero-shot classification possible: compare an image's embedding to a handful of candidate text prompts and pick the closest one, no fine-tuning required. Segment Anything (SAM) applies the same "prompt instead of retrain" philosophy to segmentation — click a point or draw a box, get a mask, for objects the model never saw labeled during training. Stack an image encoder (often CLIP's), a small projector layer, and an LLM, and you get the dominant recipe behind almost every modern vision-language model.

01Where this sits in the timeline

Vision spent a decade as a collection of separate specialties. Foundation models are the story of those specialties collapsing into a handful of reusable building blocks — the same collapse that happened to NLP once transformers arrived, just a few years later.

Before

Classification, detection, and segmentation each needed their own architecture and their own labeled dataset — a ResNet trained on ImageNet didn't transfer to Faster R-CNN's detection task or U-Net's segmentation task without substantial task-specific retraining. Every new domain (medical imaging, satellite photos, industrial defect inspection) meant collecting fresh labels and training a fresh model from something close to scratch.

→
Innovation

ViT (2020) showed attention alone, with zero convolutional inductive bias, works on images if you throw enough data and compute at it. CLIP (2021) showed contrastive pretraining on hundreds of millions of noisy web image-caption pairs produces embeddings that generalize zero-shot far beyond any single labeled dataset — no more collecting per-class labels. SAM (2023) showed one promptable model can segment anything, in domains it never trained on, given nothing but a point, box, or rough mask as a hint.

→
After

"Encoder + projector + LLM" becomes the default way to bolt vision onto a language model — image captioning, VQA, and visual reasoning systems all converge on roughly this recipe. Vision starts following the same foundation-model playbook that reshaped NLP: pretrain once at scale, adapt cheaply to everything else.

02The intuition, before any math

ViT's whole trick: stop thinking of an image as a grid of pixels a convolution slides over, and start thinking of it as a sentence made of patches.

Page 08 covers a transformer that takes a sequence of word tokens and lets every token attend to every other token. ViT doesn't invent a new mechanism for vision — it invents a new way to turn an image into a sequence, then hands that sequence to the identical transformer encoder. Cut a 224×224 image into a grid of 16×16 patches, flatten each patch into a vector, project it into an embedding, and you have a sequence of "visual words" that a transformer can process exactly like it would process a sentence. That's the entire conceptual leap — everything else is bookkeeping.

CLIP's trick is different: it isn't trying to classify anything during training. It's trying to learn a shared language between pictures and words. Feed it an image of a dog and the caption "a photo of a dog," and it nudges the image's embedding and the caption's embedding closer together in a shared space. Feed it that same image paired with an unrelated caption like "a photo of a mountain," and it pushes those embeddings apart. Do this over hundreds of millions of naturally occurring image-caption pairs scraped from the web, and the model ends up with an embedding space where "visually similar to X" and "described similarly to X" become the same notion. Once you have that, classification becomes a nearest-neighbor lookup: embed the image, embed a few candidate class names as text, see which one it's closest to.

SAM's trick is the simplest to state and the hardest to have actually built: instead of training a model to output "the segmentation mask for a cat," train a model to output "the segmentation mask for whatever the user is pointing at" — and train it on so many diverse point-mask pairs that it generalizes to objects, textures, and domains it never explicitly saw.

03Architecture: how the data actually flows

Start with ViT, since everything downstream in this page either uses it directly (CLIP's image encoder is usually a ViT) or borrows its "cut into patches, treat as tokens" trick.

ViT — turning a 224×224 image into a 197-token transformer input
A 224×224 image cut into 16×16 patches gives a 14×14 = 196-patch grid (simplified to 4×4 above). Each patch is flattened to a 768-length vector (16×16×3 channels) and linearly projected into a token embedding — the same "sequence of tokens" a transformer expects for text, just built from image patches instead of words. A learnable [CLS] token is prepended (its final-layer output is what gets classified) and position embeddings are added so the model knows where each patch sat, since attention itself has no sense of order. The 197-token sequence then runs through a standard transformer encoder, unmodified from page 08.

CLIP wraps two encoders — typically a ViT (or a CNN, in the original paper's smaller variants) for images and a transformer for text — and trains them jointly so their outputs land in the same shared embedding space:

CLIP — dual encoders trained so matching pairs land close together
Each training batch produces N image embeddings and N caption embeddings. The green diagonal cells are the true (image, caption) pairs — training pushes their cosine similarity up. Every off-diagonal cell is a mismatched pair from elsewhere in the same batch — training pushes those down. This is the same "pull matching pairs together, push everything else apart" contrastive idea used elsewhere in this curriculum, just applied across two different encoders instead of two views of one input.

SAM's architecture follows the same "encode once, prompt cheaply" shape: a heavy image encoder runs once per image and produces a rich image embedding; a lightweight prompt encoder turns a click, box, or rough mask into a prompt embedding; a small, fast mask decoder combines the two and outputs a segmentation mask — cheap enough to re-run at interactive speed every time the user gives a new prompt, without re-running the expensive image encoder.

04The equations, term by term

ViT patch embedding

$$z_0 = [\,x_{\text{class}};\ x_p^1 E;\ x_p^2 E;\ \dots;\ x_p^N E\,] + E_{pos}$$
  • x_p^i the $i$-th image patch, flattened from a $P \times P \times 3$ block of pixels into a single vector of length $P^2 \cdot 3$ — for $P=16$, that's a 768-length vector per patch.
  • E a single learned linear projection matrix, shared across every patch, mapping that 768-length vector into the model's working dimension $d_{model}$. This is the entire "convolution" ViT uses — one linear layer, applied identically to every non-overlapping patch.
  • x_class a learnable embedding prepended to the sequence, unrelated to any patch — its output after the full encoder is what the classification head reads.
  • E_pos learned position embeddings, one per sequence position, added so the model can tell patch 1 from patch 197 — otherwise the sequence is just an unordered set of patches.

The result, $z_0$, is a (197, d_model) sequence — exactly the shape a transformer encoder expects. Everything from here is identical to page 08: multi-head self-attention, residual connections, layer norm, feed-forward blocks, stacked N times.

CLIP's contrastive loss

Stated at the same level as the softmax you've already seen in 01 and 08 — this is InfoNCE, the same contrastive-loss family used elsewhere in self-supervised learning, just applied across two encoders instead of two augmented views of one input:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(I_i, T_i) / \tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp(\text{sim}(I_i, T_j) / \tau)}$$
  • sim(I,T) cosine similarity between an image embedding and a text embedding.
  • τ a learned temperature that sharpens or softens the softmax distribution over similarities.
  • N the batch size — every image in the batch is contrasted against every caption in that same batch, not just its own match.
  • symmetric loss this is computed once treating images as the anchor (image-to-text) and once treating captions as the anchor (text-to-image); CLIP trains on the average of both directions.

Bigger batches make this loss harder and more informative — each image has more negative captions to be distinguished from — which is a large part of why CLIP-style training is done at very large batch sizes.

05Why this architecture, not the obvious alternatives

The obvious alternative to ViT was "just keep using a CNN, it already works." Page 05's closing point is exactly the reason that alternative doesn't win by default: convolution bakes in locality and translation equivariance as hard architectural constraints — a kernel that detects an edge in the top-left corner detects the same edge anywhere else in the image, for free, because it's the same weights sliding across the whole input. ViT has none of that built in. A patch in the top-left and a patch in the bottom-right are just two tokens; the model has to learn from data that nearby patches tend to be related, rather than getting it handed to it by the architecture.

That's a real cost at small data scale — on ImageNet-1k alone, a ViT trained from scratch underperforms a comparably-sized ResNet, because the convolutional inductive bias acts as a strong, free regularizer and the ViT hasn't seen enough examples to learn an equivalent notion of locality on its own. The ViT paper's finding is the opposite once you pretrain on hundreds of millions of images: at that scale, the inductive bias that helped a CNN generalize from little data becomes a constraint that stops it from finding patterns a less-constrained model can discover. ViT catches up and then passes CNNs specifically as pretraining data grows — the crossover is a data/compute-scale story, not a "ViT is just better" story.

CLIP's obvious alternative was "keep training on ImageNet-style labeled datasets, just make them bigger." That doesn't scale the way natural language supervision does. A fixed label set caps out at however many classes someone was willing to define and however many images someone was willing to hand-annotate — ImageNet's 1000 classes took enormous manual effort to label. Captions, by contrast, are a byproduct of how the web already works: alt text, photo captions, and image descriptions exist in effectively unlimited free-form quantity, already paired with images, without anyone doing extra annotation work for this specific purpose. That's the entire bet behind CLIP — trade clean, expensive, fixed-vocabulary supervision for messy, free, open-vocabulary supervision, and let scale make up the difference.

Failure modes worth knowing by name

  • CLIP's zero-shot accuracy is sensitive to prompt phrasing — "dog" versus "a photo of a dog" versus "a photo of a dog, a type of animal" can shift accuracy meaningfully on the same image set, because the text encoder was trained on caption-like sentences, not bare nouns. Production zero-shot pipelines use "prompt ensembling" — averaging embeddings over several prompt templates — specifically to reduce this sensitivity.
  • SAM segments the wrong instance when a prompt is ambiguous — a single click on a person wearing a striped shirt could plausibly mean "the shirt," "the person," or "the stripe pattern," and SAM is explicitly designed to return multiple candidate masks with confidence scores in exactly this situation rather than silently guessing one.
  • VLMs (encoder + projector + LLM systems) hallucinate visual details that aren't actually in the image — describing an object, color, or count that isn't present — because the LLM component is a strong, fluent language generator that can produce a plausible-sounding sentence even when the visual evidence feeding it is thin, ambiguous, or was compressed too aggressively by the projector.
Common misconception

"ViT beats CNNs, full stop, so CNNs are obsolete." Not what the evidence shows. ViT wins when pretraining data and compute are large; at small-to-moderate scale, well-tuned CNNs remain competitive and often more data-efficient, precisely because of the inductive bias ViT lacks. Plenty of production systems in 2026 still use CNN or hybrid CNN-transformer backbones where labeled data is limited or latency is tight — "attention replaced convolution" is a scale-dependent claim, not a universal one.

07What gets built on top: grounding, video, 3D, agents

Open-vocabulary and grounding detection extend CLIP-style alignment from "classify the whole image" to "localize the region described by this free-text phrase." Instead of training a detector on a fixed list of categories (person, car, dog), these models align region-level image features with text embeddings, so a query like "the red backpack on the left" can be matched against candidate regions even if "backpack" never appeared as a training label — the model is generalizing through the same shared embedding space CLIP builds, just applied at the region level instead of the whole-image level.

Video understanding extends ViT's patch idea into time. A tubelet is a spatiotemporal patch — instead of slicing a single frame into 16×16 squares, you slice a short clip into 16×16×T chunks spanning a few frames at once, flatten each tubelet, and feed the resulting sequence through a transformer with attention operating across both space and time. This lets a single attention operation directly relate "this patch, three frames ago" to "this patch, right now" — the same one-hop, order-independent relevance-scoring idea from page 08, extended with a temporal axis.

3D vision, at a conceptual level, has two dominant representations right now. A NeRF (Neural Radiance Field) is a learned volumetric scene representation — a small neural network that takes a 3D point and a viewing direction and outputs color and density at that point, trained so that rendering rays through the network reproduces the training photos. Once trained, you can render the scene from viewpoints that were never photographed, because the network learned a continuous function over the whole volume, not just a lookup table of the input images. Gaussian splatting represents the same kind of scene explicitly instead — as a large collection of 3D Gaussian "blobs" with position, size, color, and opacity — which can be rendered by projecting and compositing those Gaussians directly, without querying a neural network per pixel per ray. That makes Gaussian splatting dramatically faster to render than a NeRF at similar visual quality, at the cost of a more literal, less compact scene representation.

Vision agents close the loop: perception (an encoder or VLM turns pixels into a description or set of detections), reasoning (an LLM decides what that perception implies and what to do next), and action (a tool call — click here, move the robot arm, crop and re-inspect this region, query a database). This perception-reasoning-action loop is the same agent architecture covered in depth on page 11, with a vision model supplying the perception step instead of, or in addition to, text.

08Vision-language models: encoder + projector + LLM

Once you have a strong image encoder (CLIP's) and a strong language model (any modern LLM), the cheapest way to combine them isn't training a new model from scratch — it's building an adapter between two models that already work. That adapter is a projector: usually a small MLP or a handful of cross-attention layers whose entire job is to map the image encoder's output features into the same embedding space the LLM's token embeddings already live in. Once the image features look, dimensionally and distributionally, like token embeddings to the LLM, you can literally concatenate them with the text prompt's token embeddings and let the LLM process both together — it doesn't need to "know" some of its input tokens originated from pixels.

The training recipe that makes this cheap: freeze or lightly fine-tune the image encoder (it's already good at producing useful visual features from CLIP-style pretraining), freeze or lightly fine-tune the LLM (it's already good at reasoning and generating fluent text), and train mostly the projector — a small, fast, comparatively cheap piece to learn — on paired image-text examples. This is why so many VLMs appear in quick succession once a good open image encoder and a good open LLM both exist: the expensive parts are reused, and only the connective tissue needs training.

This adapter-based, bolt-on-a-projector approach is different from a native multimodal architecture, where images and text are treated as first-class inputs from the start of pretraining — tokenized (or patch-embedded) and fed through a single model trained jointly on both modalities from the ground up, rather than stitching two independently-pretrained models together after the fact. Native multimodal training can in principle learn deeper cross-modal interactions, since the model never has to work around features that were optimized for a different objective; the adapter approach is cheaper, faster to iterate on, and lets you swap in a better image encoder or a better LLM independently as each one improves. Both patterns are in active production use — which one a given system uses is exactly the kind of design-tradeoff question worth being able to explain in an interview, not just recognize by name.

09Worked example and a production use case

Tiny worked example: CLIP zero-shot classification

Say you have an image and four candidate class-name prompts: "a photo of a cat", "a photo of a dog", "a photo of a car", "a photo of a bicycle". Embed the image once with CLIP's image encoder, embed all four prompts with CLIP's text encoder, and compute cosine similarity between the image embedding and each text embedding. Illustrative similarities (hand-picked, not from a real run): [0.31, 0.24, 0.09, 0.11] for cat / dog / car / bicycle respectively. Divide by temperature $\tau$ and apply softmax to turn these into a probability distribution over the four candidates — the image gets classified as whichever prompt has the highest similarity, here "cat," with no gradient update, no fine-tuning, and no cat-labeled training examples required for this specific classification task. Swap in any new list of candidate text prompts and you've defined a brand-new classifier on the spot.

Production use case

Visual search and content moderation pipelines lean on CLIP embeddings directly: embed a large catalog of images once offline, store the embeddings in a vector index, and at query time embed either a text query or a reference image and run nearest-neighbor search over that index — "find images similar to this one" and "find images matching this text description" become the same operation, a similarity search over the same embedding space. Image-captioning and visual-QA products typically use the encoder + projector + LLM pattern from the previous section: a CLIP-style image encoder plus a small projector feeding an LLM that generates the caption, answer, or reasoning trace, letting the product team swap in incrementally better open-source encoders or LLMs as they ship without retraining the whole system from scratch.

10Interview questions

BeginnerHow does ViT turn an image into a transformer input?

Cut the image into fixed-size, non-overlapping patches (16×16 is the common choice — for a 224×224 image that's 196 patches). Flatten each patch into a vector and pass it through one shared learned linear projection to get a token embedding. Prepend a learnable [CLS] token, add learned position embeddings so the model knows patch order, and feed the resulting sequence into a standard transformer encoder — identical to the one used for text.

BeginnerWhat does "promptable" mean in Segment Anything?

Instead of training a model to segment one fixed set of categories, SAM is trained to take a prompt at inference time — a point, a box, or a rough mask — and produce a segmentation mask for whatever that prompt indicates. It generalizes to objects and domains never explicitly labeled in training, because the task it learned is "segment what's pointed at," not "recognize this specific category."

IntermediateWhy does CLIP enable zero-shot classification?

CLIP's contrastive pretraining puts image embeddings and text embeddings in the same space, trained so matching image-caption pairs are close and mismatched pairs are far apart. Classification then reduces to comparing one image embedding against a handful of candidate text embeddings (one per class, phrased as a prompt like "a photo of a <class>") and picking the closest — no gradient update, no labeled examples for the new classes, because the model never learned a fixed set of output classes in the first place. It learned a general-purpose similarity function between images and language.

IntermediateWhat's the "encoder + projector + LLM" pattern, and why is it so popular?

A pretrained image encoder (often CLIP's) produces visual features; a small projector — usually an MLP or a few cross-attention layers — maps those features into the same embedding space the LLM's own token embeddings live in; the LLM then processes the projected image features alongside text tokens and does the actual reasoning and generation. It's popular because it reuses two already-expensive, already-good pretrained models and trains only the comparatively cheap connective piece, instead of pretraining a new multimodal model from scratch.

DeepWhy does ViT need more data than a CNN to reach the same accuracy, and what changes at large scale?

Convolution hard-codes locality and translation equivariance into the architecture — a kernel that detects a pattern in one location detects the same pattern anywhere, for free, because the weights are shared across every spatial position. ViT has no such constraint; a patch in one corner and a patch in another are just two tokens the model must learn to relate from data. At small data scale, that missing inductive bias hurts — the CNN's built-in regularization wins. At large pretraining scale, the same constraint that helped with little data starts limiting what patterns a CNN can discover, while ViT, unconstrained, keeps improving with more data — which is why the ViT paper's headline result only shows up once pretraining data reaches into the hundreds of millions of images, not on ImageNet-1k alone.

DeepYour CLIP-based zero-shot classifier's accuracy swings noticeably depending on the exact prompt template you use. Why does this happen, and what do you do about it?

CLIP's text encoder was trained on naturalistic, caption-like sentences scraped from the web, not on bare category names — so a prompt like "a photo of a dog" sits closer, in the learned embedding space, to how dog-related captions actually looked during training than the single word "dog" does. Sparse or unusual phrasing produces a noisier, less representative text embedding, which shows up directly as noisier similarity scores. The standard fix is prompt ensembling: embed the same class through several different prompt templates ("a photo of a {}", "a blurry photo of a {}", "a close-up photo of a {}", etc.) and average the resulting embeddings before comparing — this smooths out the sensitivity to any one specific phrasing.
