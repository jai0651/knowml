The Millennium Prize Problems

Follow the gap between checking an answer and finding one through the seven Millennium Prize Problems, and learn why P vs NP justifies gradient descent and what a machine-verified proof does and does not settle.

Here is a completed sudoku. To check it you look at each row, each column and each box and confirm that the digits 1 to 9 appear once, and that takes a minute. Now here is a blank grid with a few digits filled in. Finding the solution from there feels much slower, and for bigger puzzles of the same kind nobody knows a fast method. Whether that gap between checking an answer and finding one is real or only looks real is the oldest open question in computer science, and it's one of seven problems this chapter is about.

In 2000 the Clay Mathematics Institute named seven problems and attached $1,000,000 to each. One has been solved: Grigori Perelman proved the Poincaré conjecture and then declined the money. The other six are open. We'll look at why these seven were chosen, and then spend most of our time on the three a machine-learning reader has the most reason to care about: the sudoku question (P vs NP), the Riemann Hypothesis and the Navier–Stokes equations.

One of them is live news as this page is written. On 8 September 2026 OpenAI announced a machine-generated, formally verified proof of a blow-up result for Navier–Stokes. The Clay Institute hasn't accepted it, credit is being disputed, and section 05 walks through what was and wasn't proved.

One question will keep coming back as we go. When someone says a problem is solved, they might mean the answer was found, that it was checked, or that the community accepted it. Those are three separate events, sometimes years apart, and the same three-way split shows up whenever we evaluate a machine-learning model.

01Why a prize, and why these seven

The list was modelled on a much older one. In 1900 David Hilbert stood up in Paris and named 23 problems he thought should shape the coming century. Work on that list produced much of twentieth-century mathematics, and several of the problems turned out to be unanswerable in the form he posed them. The Clay Institute made the parallel explicit by announcing its seven problems in Paris, in May 2000, exactly a hundred years later. The money is real, but the list's main job is to say where the gaps are.

Being unsolved isn't enough to get a problem onto it, since mathematics has thousands of unsolved problems. Two further features mark these seven out. First, something large already depends on the answer. Cryptography assumes P ≠ NP, a statement we'll define in the next section. Analytic number theory routinely publishes results "assuming the Riemann Hypothesis". Physicists compute with a quantum field theory nobody has constructed rigorously. These fields didn't wait for a proof, and they moved in and built. Second, the obvious approaches are known to fail. For the deepest problems on the list there are theorems saying that whole classes of proof technique cannot work, and that is a much stronger statement than "we tried and got stuck".

Why can't you win by posting a proof?

Because the Clay rules ask for more than a correct argument. It has to be published in a peer-reviewed journal of worldwide repute, and then roughly two years have to pass during which the mathematical community generally accepts it. That gap between correct and accepted isn't bureaucracy. Wiles's first proof of Fermat's Last Theorem had a hole in it that took a year to fix. Section 05 is about what happens when a machine produces a proof faster than the community can absorb it.

With the ground rules in place, here are all seven side by side, so we can see where the three we'll study sit among the rest.

Before we move on, the finished one deserves a paragraph, because its story shows how a solution gets recognised. Grigori Perelman posted three preprints to arXiv in 2002 and 2003, completing a programme Richard Hamilton had started with Ricci flow, which deforms a shape according to its own curvature and lets you watch what it becomes. He never published in a journal, declined the Fields Medal in 2006, and declined the million dollars in 2010. Mathematicians accepted the proof anyway, so a proof can be settled without following the Clay process.

Now for the problem on the list that touches machine learning every day, whether or not anyone mentions it. It's the one the sudoku was pointing at.

03P vs NP, and why it is in your loss function

Back to the sudoku. Some problems are easy to check and appear hard to solve: given a completed grid you can verify it in seconds, and given a blank one you can't solve it nearly as fast. Computer scientists make this precise by asking how the work grows as the problem gets bigger. A problem is in P if it can be solved in polynomial time, meaning the number of steps grows like some fixed power of the input size, such as its square or its cube. A problem is in NP if a proposed solution can be verified in polynomial time. Every problem in P is also in NP, because if you can solve something quickly you can certainly check an answer quickly. The open question is whether the two classes are the same, so that every problem with a quickly checkable answer also has a quickly findable one.

Almost everyone believes P ≠ NP, meaning that finding is harder than checking. Nobody can prove it, and three separate results explain why the obvious attacks fail, which makes this more than a gap in the literature. First came relativization (Baker, Gill and Solovay, 1975). Imagine giving every machine access to an oracle, a black box that answers some hard question instantly. Baker, Gill and Solovay showed there are oracles that make P = NP and others that make P ≠ NP. So any proof that would still work after you hand the machines an oracle can't settle the question.

Researchers then turned to arguments about circuits, which are networks of logic gates, and tried to prove that certain problems need very large circuits. That approach had produced the field's best lower bounds, so it looked promising. Natural proofs (Razborov and Rudich, 1994) showed that a large family of such circuit-complexity arguments provably cannot separate P from NP unless strong cryptography is impossible. A third result closed the remaining door. A technique had been found that got around relativization, and algebrization (Aaronson and Wigderson, 2008) showed that it runs into a barrier of its own.

Why does this matter if you only train neural networks?

Because training a neural network to its global optimum, the very best setting of all its weights, is NP-hard in general. That has been known since Blum and Rivest showed it for a network with three nodes in 1992. Non-convex optimisation isn't a temporary embarrassment waiting for a smarter algorithm. So the field does something specific and defensible, and stops asking for the optimum. Gradient descent finds a good-enough local solution, and the whole practice of machine learning is built on the observation that good-enough generalises well. Complexity theory tells us this is a sound trade, and not a way of giving up.

The same logic runs through the rest of the stack. Exact inference in a general graphical model is intractable, so we sample. Optimal decoding over all possible sequences is intractable, so we use beam search and accept that it's greedy. Many of the heuristics you've learned exist because somebody proved the exact version is out of reach.

P vs NP is a question about what's possible in principle. Our next problem comes with a different kind of evidence: a great deal of data and still no proof.

04The Riemann Hypothesis: a load-bearing conjecture

The Riemann Hypothesis is the clearest example on the list of a field that decided not to wait. It starts with a function, the Riemann zeta function $\zeta(s)$, and with the values of $s$ where it equals zero. Some of those zeros are boring and easy to locate. The rest, the non-trivial zeros, are conjectured to lie exactly on the vertical line where the real part of $s$ is one half:

$$\zeta(s) = 0 \ \Longrightarrow\ \operatorname{Re}(s) = \tfrac{1}{2} \quad \text{(for non-trivial zeros)}$$

Stated in 1859, in an eight-page paper, almost in passing.

This sounds like a technicality about one function. It matters because the zeros of $\zeta$ control how irregularly the prime numbers are distributed. The Prime Number Theorem tells you roughly how many primes lie below a given size. The Riemann Hypothesis would tell you how far off "roughly" can be, and it would be the tightest possible answer.

Its status is odd, because two facts have to be held together. Numerically the evidence is overwhelming: something on the order of ten trillion zeros have been checked, and every one is on the line. And that evidence proves nothing. Ten trillion isn't a proof, and mathematicians have been burned before by patterns that hold for enormous ranges and then fail. An analogous statement over finite fields, the Weil conjectures, has been proved. That is encouraging, and not the same thing.

Why do papers build on something nobody has proved?

Because waiting would mean waiting indefinitely, and the conditional result is still a result: it says what follows if the conjecture holds. So hundreds of published theorems begin with the phrase "assume the Riemann Hypothesis", and an entire literature is conditional on an unproved statement. Trouble comes if the hypothesis ever fails. That literature wouldn't merely lose a tool, because parts of it would become false.

Both problems so far are old and quiet. Our next problem is the opposite, since a claim about it appeared as this page was being written.

05Navier–Stokes, and the claim of 8 September 2026

This section is a snapshot of a story that is one day old as it's written. Treat every part of it as provisional and check the sources.

The Navier–Stokes equations describe how a fluid moves. They're used to design aircraft, forecast weather and model blood flow, and they work. What nobody can prove is that they always make sense. Start with a smooth, finite-energy flow and run the equations forward in time. Does the solution stay smooth forever, or can the velocity become infinite somewhere in finite time? That failure is called a singularity, or a blow-up. If the equations blow up, they aren't a complete description of a real fluid, because real water doesn't reach infinite speed.

What the prize asks for

Charles Fefferman wrote the official problem statement, and its structure matters a great deal for reading the news. The prize is offered for proving any one of four statements, two in each direction. Statements (A) and (B) are about smoothness: prove that solutions exist and stay smooth for all time, with no external force, in ordinary three-dimensional space (A) or in a periodic box (B). Statements (C) and (D) are about breakdown: exhibit a solution that blows up. For these two the rules permit a smooth external force, provided it satisfies the decay and smoothness conditions Fefferman specifies.

Why does a blow-up with an outside force still count?

Because the official statement says so. A blow-up example with an admissible smooth forcing term is one of the four things the prize was explicitly offered for, so the forced case isn't a dodge.

What was announced

On 8 September 2026 OpenAI published a claimed proof of a blow-up result, aiming at the breakdown branch. Its reported process is as striking as the result. On the order of ten thousand agents worked in parallel for about 88 hours, and the final argument was written in Lean and machine-checked.

Lean is a proof assistant, a program that checks that every step of a proof follows from stated axioms with no gaps. That's a much stronger guarantee than a referee reading prose, and if Lean accepts a proof, the theorem as written is correct. What Lean can't check is whether the theorem as written is the theorem you meant. Translating Fefferman's conditions into formal statements is human work, and whether a given formalisation faithfully captures the prize problem is a judgement call that a compiler can't make. This is the crux of the current argument.

Why it isn't settled

Start with the official side. The Clay Institute hasn't accepted the claim, and Navier–Stokes is still listed as unsolved. CMI's president, Martin Bridson, has said the evaluation will be "deliberately unhurried" and "absolutely rigorous", and under the rules in section 01, acceptance was never going to be quick. OpenAI says it isn't seeking the prize, which sidesteps the process and doesn't pass through it.

Credit is also disputed. Tristan Buckmaster of NYU and Levent Alpöge of Anthropic had been working the same ground and announced a related Euler result immediately beforehand. Buckmaster has raised the possibility that OpenAI's models benefited from his unpublished work through a product he had used. OpenAI denies using their prompts or proofs, while acknowledging it can't fully rule out that de-identified usage data influenced its models.

Finally, the underlying mathematics isn't the machine's. Fefferman himself has pointed to Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa as "the heroes of the story" for the analytic techniques that made this line of attack possible. Córdoba's own reaction to the announcement was that if it holds, it is "a big surprise for us".

The honest summary, on 9 September 2026, is that a machine produced a formally verified proof of a serious theorem in a field where progress is measured in decades, building on human techniques developed over the previous three years, and the community hasn't yet decided what it amounts to. That's remarkable, and it differs from saying "AI solved a Millennium Problem". Before we ask what the machines are good at, we should finish the list.

06The other three, briefly

Three problems are left, and they sit further from this site's subject matter. Each is a bridge between two areas, and that is why they made the list, so here is what each one connects.

Yang–Mills existence and mass gap

The strong force is described by a quantum field theory that physicists compute with constantly. The problem is to construct that theory rigorously on four-dimensional space, and prove it has a mass gap: a strictly positive lowest excitation energy.

Why it matters: the mass gap is why the strong force has short range even though gluons are massless. Lattice simulations show it clearly. Nobody has built the theory they are simulating.

Hodge conjecture

On a smooth projective complex variety, certain classes identified by topology are conjectured to always come from actual algebraic subvarieties, up to rational coefficients.

Why it matters: it is a bridge between topology and algebra. Proving it would say that a shape's holes are always cut out by equations, and that is the kind of statement that reorganises a field.

Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer

For an elliptic curve over the rationals, the conjecture says the rank of its group of rational points equals the order of vanishing of its L-function at $s=1$.

Why it matters: it connects counting solutions to an analytic object. Special cases are proved, notably for rank 0 and 1 under conditions, via work of Gross, Zagier and Kolyvagin. The general case is open.

Notice that none of these three asks us to search for an example. They ask for a new way of understanding a structure, and that observation matters for the next section, where we ask what machines can do with such problems.

07Where AI touches this

The useful question isn't whether machines will solve these problems. It's which kind of mathematical work they're currently good at, because the answer is specific and it generalises far beyond mathematics. The last three years produced a real track record, so we'll walk through it in the order that each piece depends on the one before.

It starts with infrastructure. Lean and its library mathlib let a proof be machine-checked line by line, and everything else here depends on this substrate, which humans built. On top of it came competition mathematics. DeepMind's AlphaProof, trained with reinforcement learning over auto-formalised problems, reached silver-medal level at the 2024 International Mathematical Olympiad. In 2025 both DeepMind and OpenAI reported gold-medal-level performance, five problems out of six, using general-purpose reasoning models.

Next came finding constructions, meaning objects with a stated property. FunSearch improved a bound on the cap set problem. AlphaEvolve found a way to multiply two 4×4 matrices with 48 scalar multiplications, beating the 49 that Strassen's method had held since 1969. Most recently, an AI agent resolved a small number of formalised open Erdős problems, on the order of nine out of several hundred attempted, with human experts checking that the formal statements matched the originals.

Look at what those wins have in common. A 48-multiplication scheme is hard to find and trivial to check. A Lean proof is hard to construct and mechanically checkable. An IMO problem has an answer you can grade. This is the shape of problem current AI is strong on: an enormous search space with cheap, reliable verification. Where a verifier exists, you can let a model search badly and often, and throw away everything that fails.

Why can't the same method attack the Riemann Hypothesis?

Because nobody is attacking the Riemann Hypothesis, Hodge or Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer this way, because of what they appear to need. They don't look like a search through candidate objects. They look like they need a new idea about what the right objects are, and there's no verifier for that. Page 37 shows the same dividing line running through every industry. This is also where the sudoku from the start of the chapter returns: checking is cheap, finding is the hard part, and machines help most where checking is cheap.

That leaves one skill to build, which is reading the headlines about these results without being misled.

08What breaks

Mostly it's the reporting. The phrase "AI solved a Millennium Problem" collapses three different claims, because a proof existing, a proof being verified, and a proof being accepted as resolving the prize problem are separate events, sometimes years apart. And even a verified proof can miss the point. Lean guarantees the argument is sound, but whether the formal statement is the intended statement is a human judgement, and that is exactly where the current dispute sits.

The machine's contribution is also easy to overstate. The Navier–Stokes attack rests on analytic techniques developed by named humans over years, and the model did a hard, specific piece of work on top of that foundation. Numerical evidence is easy to overread too, and this bites in ML as well. Ten trillion zeros on the line proves nothing about the next one, and a benchmark passing on ten thousand examples proves nothing about the distribution you haven't sampled.

Two last cautions concern the list itself. "Unsolved" doesn't mean "untouched", since every problem here has a large partial literature, and special cases, analogues and reformulations are where the progress lives. And prizes distort attention: plenty of consequential open problems carry no million dollars, so the list is a snapshot of what seemed important in 2000 and not a ranking of importance today.

09Summary

  1. The Clay list names where the gaps are. Seven problems were announced in 2000, a hundred years after Hilbert's 23, with $1,000,000 each, and only the Poincaré conjecture is solved.
  2. A problem earns a place by being load-bearing. Whole fields already depend on the answer, and for the deepest problems there are theorems showing that the obvious proof techniques fail.
  3. Correct and accepted are different things. The Clay rules need journal publication and about two years of general acceptance, and Wiles's first Fermat proof had a hole that took a year to fix.
  4. P vs NP asks whether checking is as easy as finding. Relativization, natural proofs and algebrization each rule out a family of proof techniques.
  5. P ≠ NP justifies gradient descent. Reaching a neural network's global optimum is NP-hard, so settling for a good local solution is a sound trade, and the same goes for sampling and beam search.
  6. The Riemann Hypothesis shows that evidence isn't proof. About ten trillion zeros sit on the line, and hundreds of theorems still begin "assume the Riemann Hypothesis".
  7. Navier–Stokes is asked in four forms. Statements (A) and (B) want global smoothness with no force, and (C) and (D) want a blow-up, where a smooth admissible force is allowed.
  8. Lean guarantees soundness, not relevance. A verified proof is correct as written, but whether the formal statement is the intended problem is a human judgement, and that's the crux of the September 2026 dispute.
  9. The September 2026 claim isn't settled. Clay hasn't accepted it, credit is disputed, and the analytic techniques it rests on came from named humans such as Córdoba and Martínez-Zoroa.
  10. AI is strong where a search space is huge and verification is cheap. That covers the 48-multiplication matrix scheme, Lean proofs and Olympiad problems, and it explains why Riemann, Hodge and Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer, which need new concepts, aren't being attacked this way.

10Interview questions

BeginnerWhat does P vs NP ask, and why does it matter for machine learning?

P is the class of problems solvable in polynomial time; NP is the class whose candidate solutions can be verified in polynomial time. The question is whether the two classes coincide, and the near-universal expectation is that they do not. It matters for machine learning because the exact versions of many things we do are provably intractable: finding the global optimum of a neural network's loss is NP-hard, exact inference in general graphical models is intractable, and optimal sequence decoding is infeasible. That is the justification for the entire toolkit of approximations we actually use, from gradient descent to sampling to beam search. Complexity theory is what tells you those are principled trades rather than shortcuts.

BeginnerWhich Millennium Prize Problem has been solved, and by whom?

The Poincaré conjecture, which asks whether every simply connected closed three-dimensional manifold is homeomorphic to the three-sphere. Grigori Perelman proved it in a series of arXiv preprints in 2002 and 2003, completing a programme based on Richard Hamilton's Ricci flow, which deforms a manifold according to its own curvature. He never submitted the work to a journal, declined the Fields Medal in 2006 and declined the million-dollar prize in 2010. The other six problems remain open, with a disputed claim on Navier–Stokes as of September 2026.

IntermediateWhy do complexity-theoretic barriers make P vs NP different from an ordinary open problem?

Because there are theorems ruling out entire families of proof technique. Relativization, from Baker, Gill and Solovay in 1975, shows that any argument which still works when machines are given an oracle cannot resolve the question, since some oracles make the classes equal and others separate them. Natural proofs, from Razborov and Rudich in 1994, shows that a broad class of circuit lower-bound arguments cannot separate P from NP unless strong pseudorandom functions fail to exist. Algebrization, from Aaronson and Wigderson in 2008, shows the main technique that evaded relativization has its own limit. Together they mean progress requires a genuinely new kind of argument, which is a much stronger statement than nobody having found the proof yet.

IntermediateWhat does it mean for the Navier–Stokes equations to "blow up", and why would that matter?

Blow-up means a solution that starts smooth with finite energy develops a singularity in finite time, with velocity becoming unbounded at some point. It matters because it would show the equations are an incomplete model of a real fluid: water does not reach infinite speed, so a blow-up marks the point where the mathematics stops describing physics. It also matters for the prize specifically, because Fefferman's official statement offers the award for either direction. Two of the four statements ask for global smoothness with no external force, and the other two ask for a breakdown example, where a smooth forcing term satisfying stated conditions is permitted.

IntermediateA model produces a Lean-verified proof of an open conjecture. What can and cannot be concluded?

You can conclude that the formal statement follows from the axioms with no gaps, which is a stronger correctness guarantee than a human referee provides. What you cannot conclude is that the formalised statement is the conjecture people care about. Translating an informal problem into a formal one involves choices about definitions, quantifiers and side conditions, and a faithful translation is a human judgement rather than something the proof checker validates. So the remaining work after verification is a mathematical and social process: experts confirm the statement is the intended one, and the community absorbs the argument. This is exactly the gap at issue in the September 2026 Navier–Stokes claim.

DeepWhat kinds of mathematical problem is current AI good at, and why?

Problems with an enormous search space and a cheap, reliable verifier. Finding a scheme that multiplies 4×4 matrices in 48 multiplications is hard to discover and trivial to check, which is why AlphaEvolve could improve on a bound that had stood since 1969. A Lean proof is hard to construct and mechanically checkable. Competition problems have gradeable answers. In all these cases a model can search unreliably and at volume, discarding everything that fails verification. The problems where this does not apply are the ones needing a new conceptual framework rather than a search over candidate objects, which is the situation for the Riemann Hypothesis, Hodge and Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer. There is no verifier for "is this the right definition", so there is nothing to search against.

DeepHundreds of papers assume the Riemann Hypothesis. Is that sound practice?

It is standard and it is explicitly flagged, which is what makes it defensible. A conditional theorem is a real result: it establishes an implication, and it maps out what would follow if the conjecture holds. The evidence base is also unusually strong, with on the order of ten trillion zeros verified on the critical line and a proved analogue over finite fields. The risk is nonetheless real and asymmetric. If the hypothesis fails, conditional results do not merely lose a convenience; some of them become false, and the error terms that depend on it are exactly the quantitative content of the work. The practice is sound because the dependency is declared, not because the assumption is safe.
