Unsupervised & Self-Supervised

Two different answers to "how do you learn from data with no labels" — find the structure that's already there, or invent a supervised task out of the data itself. Interviewers test whether you know which is which.

Know conceptually ~24 min · prerequisites: eigenvectors, SVD & mutual information (01) — the encoder/backprop machinery used informally here gets its full treatment in 04
TL;DR

Unsupervised learning finds structure in data with no labels anywhere in the loop — clustering, dimensionality reduction, density estimation. Self-supervised learning is a specific strategy inside that same unlabeled-data world: manufacture a supervised-looking task directly from the data itself — mask a word and predict it, make two crops of an image and decide they're the same thing — so you get a real loss function and a real gradient without a human ever providing a label. Almost every foundation model trained since 2018 starts with a self-supervised pretraining phase. One sentence for an interview: unsupervised learning asks "what structure is already here?"; self-supervised learning asks "what free prediction task can I invent from this data to force a network to learn something useful?" — and conflating the two is one of the most common stumbles in this area.

01Where this sits in the timeline

Self-supervised learning didn't replace unsupervised learning — it's a newer idea that grew up alongside it, aimed at a problem classical unsupervised methods were never built to solve: producing representations a downstream neural network can actually reuse.

Before

For decades, "no labels" meant classical clustering and linear dimensionality reduction: k-means partitioning customers into segments, PCA compressing hand-engineered features into a handful of components, hierarchical clustering building a dendrogram of documents or species. Genuinely useful for exploration, compression, and visualization — but built on features you already had to design by hand, and none of it produces representations you can hand to a downstream network and expect to transfer.

→
Innovation

Two pretext tasks, invented independently, showed the same principle in different modalities. BERT (2018) masks words in a sentence and trains a model to predict just those — no labels, just the text supplying its own supervision. SimCLR and MoCo (2020) take two random augmentations of the same image and train an encoder so their embeddings land close together while embeddings of different images land far apart. Different mechanics, same insight: you can manufacture a real, gradient-bearing training signal from unlabeled data, and the representations that fall out of solving that invented task turn out to be genuinely useful downstream.

→
After

By the mid-2020s, self-supervised pretraining isn't an alternative to the standard pipeline — it is the standard pipeline. GPT-style models pretrain on next-token prediction, a self-supervised objective at massive scale. BERT-style encoders pretrain on masked prediction. CLIP pretrains by contrasting matched image-text pairs against mismatched ones — the same joint-embedding idea as SimCLR, applied across two modalities instead of two augmentations. Labeled fine-tuning, where it happens at all, is now a small, cheap final step on top of representations mostly learned before a single human label was involved.

02The intuition, before any math

The single most common interview trap in this area: "unsupervised" and "self-supervised" are not the same thing, even though both work on unlabeled data.

Unsupervised learning has no notion of a target at all. Feed k-means a pile of customer purchase vectors and it partitions them into groups — there's no correct grouping it's checking its answer against, no loss function comparing a prediction to a ground truth. The entire goal is to expose structure that's already sitting in the data: clusters, a lower-dimensional manifold, a density estimate.

Self-supervised learning still has zero human-provided labels, but it does have a target — the model just generates that target itself, from the data, and trains with an ordinary supervised loss against it. Take a sentence, hide one word, and ask a model to predict the hidden word from the rest of the sentence: the label is just the word you already had and chose to hide. Take one image, produce two randomly cropped and color-jittered versions of it, and ask a model to recognize they came from the same source: the label is just the fact that you know which crops came from which image, because you're the one who made them. Both cases are completely ordinary gradient descent on a completely ordinary cross-entropy or contrastive loss — the only unusual part is where the label came from.

Why the distinction matters in an interview: if someone asks how you'd learn from images you have no labels for, the honest answer branches immediately. If you just want to explore or compress what you already have, that's an unsupervised question — reach for PCA or clustering. If you want a representation that transfers to a downstream task you haven't seen yet, that's almost always a self-supervised question now — reach for a contrastive or masked-prediction pretext task, because those are the objectives empirically shown to produce features that generalize.

There's a unifying way to think about what a good learned representation is actually doing, covered from the information-theory side in 01: a representation should keep high mutual information with whatever you actually care about downstream, while discarding nuisance variation — sensor noise, the exact pixel values of a background, the specific synonym a sentence happened to use. Every method on this page — clustering, PCA, contrastive learning, masked prediction — is a different strategy for approximating that same goal without ever being told explicitly what "what you care about" is.

03Architecture: how the data actually flows

This section is a tour, not one deep-dive — "unsupervised and self-supervised" spans a wide family of methods that don't share a single architecture. Group them by what they're actually doing and the space gets much smaller: partition or model the data directly (clustering), find a lower-dimensional description of it (dimensionality reduction), compress and reconstruct it (autoencoders), or invent a prediction task and solve it with an encoder (self-supervised learning). That last bucket is where most of 2020s representation-learning research lives, so it gets the most depth, and the one diagram.

Classical clustering: k-means, k-medoids, hierarchical, DBSCAN

k-means starts by picking k initial cluster centers (centroids), then alternates two steps until nothing changes: assign every point to its nearest centroid, then recompute each centroid as the mean of the points now assigned to it. It converges — total within-cluster distance can only decrease or stay flat each round — but not necessarily to the best possible partition. A bad random initialization can trap it in a poor local optimum, which is why in practice you run it from several starting points (or use k-means++, a smarter initialization that spreads the starting centroids out) and keep the best result. k-medoids solves the same problem but forces each cluster's center to be an actual data point — a medoid — rather than a computed mean, which makes it far less sensitive to outliers: one extreme point can drag a k-means centroid a long way, but it can't become the medoid unless it's genuinely central.

Hierarchical (agglomerative) clustering skips picking k at the start entirely. It begins with every point as its own cluster, then repeatedly merges the two closest clusters — by some linkage rule: closest pair of points, farthest pair, or average distance across all pairs — until everything is one cluster. The result is a dendrogram, a tree recording every merge, and you choose k after the fact by cutting the tree at whatever height gives you the number of groups you want. The cost is that it doesn't scale the way k-means does — computing and updating all pairwise distances is quadratic in the number of points in the naive form.

DBSCAN drops the idea of a cluster center altogether and defines clusters by density instead: a point is a core point if enough other points (minPts) sit within a small radius (eps) of it, and clusters grow by chaining together core points that are close to each other. Points that don't belong to any dense region get labeled noise instead of being forced into a cluster they don't belong in. That buys two things k-means structurally cannot: clusters of arbitrary shape, not just roughly spherical blobs, and automatic outlier detection, with no need to specify k in advance. The tradeoff is that eps and minPts aren't always easy to pick, and DBSCAN struggles badly when different real clusters in the same dataset have very different densities — one eps can't be simultaneously right for a tight cluster and a sparse one.

Gaussian mixture models and EM

A Gaussian Mixture Model is what you get if you make k-means probabilistic. Instead of assuming every cluster is a hard, spherical blob with every point belonging to exactly one of them, a GMM assumes the data was generated by a mixture of k Gaussians, each with its own mean, covariance (so clusters can be elliptical, not just round), and mixing weight — and every point gets a soft, probabilistic membership across all k of them instead of a hard assignment to one.

You can't fit a GMM's parameters with a simple closed-form formula, because you don't know which Gaussian generated which point and you don't know the Gaussians' parameters — each depends on the other. Expectation-Maximization breaks that circularity by alternating, exactly like k-means, just with soft numbers instead of hard ones. The E-step fixes the current parameters and computes, for every point, the responsibility — the posterior probability it came from each of the k Gaussians, given where the Gaussians currently sit. The M-step fixes those responsibilities and re-fits each Gaussian's mean, covariance, and mixing weight, using the responsibilities as per-point soft weights instead of hard 0/1 membership. Repeat — assign softly, then re-fit — and the data's likelihood under the model provably increases every round until it converges. k-means is the special case you get by forcing every Gaussian to be spherical, tiny, and equally weighted: the soft responsibilities collapse into hard 0/1 assignments, and the M-step's weighted mean becomes an ordinary mean. Same alternating logic — one is just a limit of the other. Like k-means, EM only guarantees a local optimum of the likelihood, not the global one, so in practice you run it from several starting points (often seeded with a quick k-means pass) and keep the best result by final log-likelihood.

Dimensionality reduction: PCA, ICA, NMF, spectral methods

PCA, ICA, and NMF all answer the same question — can this high-dimensional data be described with far fewer numbers — with three different notions of what makes a good small description. The eigenvector and SVD machinery behind PCA is covered in full in 01 (the Essence of Linear Algebra series is the best visual build-up of that machinery if eigenvectors still feel abstract); here's the recap plus the two variants that differ in what they actually optimize for.

PCA finds the orthogonal directions of maximum variance in the data and projects onto the top few — it decorrelates the data, and it's the optimal linear projection if the only goal is preserving variance, equivalently minimizing squared reconstruction error. ICA (Independent Component Analysis) asks for something strictly stronger than decorrelation: statistically independent components, not merely uncorrelated ones. That distinction matters because decorrelated is a much weaker condition than independent — two variables can have zero correlation and still be tightly, nonlinearly dependent. ICA needs the underlying sources to be non-Gaussian to be identifiable at all, and it's the tool behind the classic cocktail-party problem: given several microphone recordings, each a different mixture of the same underlying voices, recover the original, independent voice signals. NMF (Non-negative Matrix Factorization) adds a different constraint instead — every factor and every coefficient must be non-negative — which sounds like a small restriction but has a big effect: because components can only add, never cancel, NMF tends to discover parts-based, additive representations (facial features that combine into a face, topics that combine into a document) that are often far more interpretable than PCA's components, which mix positive and negative contributions and are hard to read individually.

Spectral methods take a different starting point: build a graph where nearby data points are connected, weighted by similarity, compute that graph's Laplacian, and use its eigenvectors as a new coordinate system for the data. Spectral clustering then runs ordinary k-means in that new coordinate system instead of raw feature space, which lets it correctly separate clusters that are non-convex or intertwined — two concentric rings, say — that k-means would butcher directly, since k-means only knows how to draw straight-line, Euclidean boundaries.

Autoencoders, denoising autoencoders, and VAEs

A plain autoencoder is an encoder-decoder pair trained end to end to reconstruct its own input through a bottleneck: compress x down to a low-dimensional code z, then decode z back to something close to x, minimizing reconstruction error. It's unsupervised in the sense defined above — no labels, just x predicting x — but its latent space has no guaranteed structure beyond whatever shape makes reconstruction loss small. That's exactly why a plain autoencoder isn't a generative model: sample a random point in its latent space and, unless it happens to land near where a real training example landed, the decoder produces garbage, because nothing during training ever asked the latent space to be smooth or densely packed.

A denoising autoencoder makes one small, effective change: corrupt the input — mask patches, add noise — before it reaches the encoder, but still ask the decoder to reconstruct the clean, uncorrupted original. The network can no longer get away with learning something close to the identity function; it has to learn the actual structure and correlations in the data, since the corrupted or masked parts have to be inferred from everything else. This is the same underlying idea — corrupt, then predict the clean version — that reappears further down this page as BERT-style masked language modeling and MAE-style masked image modeling: same mechanism, different modality.

A Variational Autoencoder fixes the plain autoencoder's ungenerative latent space directly. Instead of encoding x to a single point z, the encoder outputs the parameters of a distribution over z — a mean and variance — a sample is drawn from that distribution, and a KL-divergence term in the loss pulls every input's latent distribution toward a fixed, simple prior, almost always a standard Gaussian. That regularization term is the entire trick: it forces the latent space to be smooth and densely packed with no gaps, so sampling from the prior — not just encoding a real example — reliably decodes into something plausible. The full latent-variable setup, and the ELBO derivation behind it, gets its complete treatment in 12; the equation itself is recapped below.

Self-supervised learning: manufacturing a task, then solving it with an encoder

Everything from here on is self-supervised in the specific sense from the intuition section: no human label, but a real, invented target. There are two broad families, and the distinction between them — joint-embedding versus generative objectives, in the curriculum's own language — matters more than the list of model names attached to each.

Joint-embedding methods are the ones this page's diagram covers, and contrastive learning is the clearest version of the idea. Take one unlabeled image. Apply two different random augmentations to it — a random crop, a color jitter, a horizontal flip, whatever the pipeline defines — and you get two different-looking images that are still, semantically, the same underlying thing. Run both through the same encoder — literally the same weights, applied twice — to get two embeddings. Those two embeddings are a positive pair, and the training objective pulls them together. Every other image in the same training batch, run through that same encoder, gives embeddings that are negatives — the objective pushes those apart. No human ever labeled anything; the label is just "these two crops came from the same source image and these others didn't," a fact you know for free because you generated the crops yourself.

Contrastive learning — two augmented views, one shared encoder, pulled together vs. pushed apart
Two different random augmentations of the same source image pass through the identical, shared-weight encoder f(·), producing embeddings z₁ and z₂ — the positive pair. Every other image's embedding in the same batch (n₁, n₂, n₃) is a negative. InfoNCE pulls the positive pair's similarity up and pushes every negative's similarity down, simultaneously, with every image in the batch taking a turn as the anchor.

This whole setup — two or more branches sharing weights, trained so that distance in embedding space means something — is a Siamese network, and it predates SimCLR by decades; it's the same architecture behind face-verification systems (are these two photos the same person?) and one-shot signature or fingerprint matching. Metric learning is the general name for training an embedding space where distance is meaningful this way, usually with a contrastive loss (pull positives together, push negatives apart by at least some margin) or a triplet loss (anchor, positive, negative — push the anchor-negative distance to exceed the anchor-positive distance by a margin).

SimCLR (2020) is close to the diagram above in its purest form: large batches supply the negatives directly, and augmentation strategy — not architecture — turned out to be the biggest single lever in the original paper's own ablations. MoCo (2020) gets a large number of negatives without needing enormous batches, by keeping a running queue of embeddings from recent batches, computed by a slowly-updated momentum encoder, as a large, cheap negative pool. BYOL and SimSiam, both 2020, then asked a genuinely surprising question: do you need negative pairs at all? Both train with only positive pairs and still avoid collapsing to a trivial solution — how, and why that's a nontrivial claim, is worth its own discussion in Failure modes, below.

The other broad family generates its supervision by masking, then reconstructing, in input space rather than embedding space. BERT-style masked language modeling (2018) hides about 15% of the tokens in a sentence and trains the model to predict them from the surrounding context — bidirectionally, since the whole point is that the model can look both left and right of the masked token, something a left-to-right autoregressive model can't do. MAE-style masked image modeling (2021) does the visual analogue: mask out a large majority of an image's patches — MAE's own default is a striking 75% — and train a decoder to reconstruct the missing pixels from the small number of visible patches an encoder was allowed to see. That very high masking ratio is deliberate: images have far more spatial redundancy than text, so a much more aggressive mask is needed to make the reconstruction task actually hard enough to force useful learning, rather than something solvable by copying nearby pixels.

Contrastive and masked-prediction methods are both self-supervised, but they differ in exactly where the prediction target lives. Joint-embedding methods — SimCLR, MoCo, BYOL, SimSiam — never reconstruct anything in input space; the entire objective lives in embedding space, comparing representations to each other. Generative or reconstruction methods — denoising autoencoders, BERT, MAE — predict directly in the original input space: actual tokens, actual pixels. The practical tradeoff: reconstruction objectives are simple and stable to train — it's ordinary regression or classification against a well-defined target, and a trivial constant-output encoder genuinely fails at reconstructing real detail, so collapse isn't really a live risk — but they can spend a lot of model capacity getting low-level detail right that may not matter for a downstream task; the exact texture of grass in the background doesn't matter for classifying "dog." Joint-embedding objectives sidestep wasting capacity on irrelevant detail, since they only ever have to make the right high-level things end up close together, but that freedom is exactly what opens the door to the failure mode covered next: representation collapse.

04The equations, term by term

InfoNCE, term by term

InfoNCE (introduced for representation learning by van den Oord et al., 2018, and the loss SimCLR uses directly — sometimes called NT-Xent, normalized temperature-scaled cross-entropy) turns the pull/push picture from the diagram above into one loss function. For a positive pair (i, j) drawn from a batch of 2N augmented examples — N images, two augmentations each:

$$\ell_{i,j} = -\log \frac{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(z_i,z_j)/\tau)}{\sum_{k=1}^{2N} \mathbb{1}_{[k \neq i]}\, \exp(\mathrm{sim}(z_i,z_k)/\tau)}$$
  • z_i, z_j the two embeddings of the positive pair — same source image, two different augmentations — both L2-normalized so their dot product is a cosine similarity.
  • sim(·,·) cosine similarity between two embeddings, ranging from -1 to 1: how aligned the two vectors are.
  • τ temperature, a scalar that rescales similarities before the softmax. Small τ sharpens the distribution so the loss reacts much more strongly to hard, nearby negatives; large τ flattens it so every negative contributes more evenly. The worked example below makes this concrete with numbers.
  • numerator the positive pair's similarity, exponentiated — the one term gradient descent is trying to make as large as possible relative to everything else.
  • denominator the sum over every other embedding in the batch except i itself — normalizing against the whole batch is what pushes every negative's similarity down, since increasing the numerator's share of that sum requires decreasing everyone else's relative share.

The ELBO, recapped

A Variational Autoencoder's loss is built from the evidence lower bound, or ELBO — the standard variational-inference objective from Kingma & Welling (2013), and what makes maximizing an otherwise-intractable log p(x) practical to train by gradient descent. The full derivation, and the reparameterization trick that makes sampling z differentiable, lives in 12 — here's the equation and what each term is doing:

$$\log p(x) \geq \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x|z)\big] - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\phi(z|x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)$$
  • q_φ(z|x) the encoder's output distribution over latents given the input — typically a Gaussian whose mean and variance are predicted by the encoder network.
  • E[log p_θ(x|z)] expected log-likelihood of reconstructing x from a sampled z — in practice, this is just the reconstruction loss.
  • p(z) the fixed prior being regularized toward, almost always a standard Gaussian.
  • D_KL(·‖·) the penalty for how far the encoder's per-input distribution strays from that prior. This is the term a plain autoencoder doesn't have, and it's exactly what makes the latent space smooth and samplable.

05Why self-supervised pretraining won, not just "get more labels"

The obvious alternative to inventing a pretext task is labeling more data, and it doesn't scale the way self-supervision does — mostly for economic reasons, not algorithmic ones. Raw text, images, audio, and video are abundant; the internet produces them continuously at a cost close to zero per example. Labels are not: every one requires a human decision, at a rate and cost that scales with dataset size. Doubling an unlabeled dataset is usually a storage and compute problem. Doubling a labeled dataset is a headcount and time problem, and those don't scale the way GPUs do.

Self-supervised pretraining sidesteps the bottleneck by getting the model to extract structure from the raw, abundant data first — what a well-formed sentence looks like, what a real natural image looks like, what tends to co-occur with what — before a single labeled example is involved. Fine-tuning on labels then only has to teach the specific task on top of representations that already understand the domain, which is why fine-tuning typically needs orders of magnitude fewer labeled examples than training the same architecture from a random initialization would. It's also why label-quality issues — inconsistent annotators, ambiguous categories, ordinary noise in any large human-labeled dataset — matter less than they used to: the representation doing most of the heavy lifting was never trained on those noisy labels in the first place.

This doesn't make labels obsolete — you still need them to point the model at the specific task you actually want and to check whether pretraining worked at all. What changed is the ratio: a small, carefully curated labeled set on top of a good self-supervised backbone now reliably beats a much larger labeled set feeding a model trained from scratch, and that ratio shift is the practical reason this happened as fast as it did.

06Failure modes, evaluation, and what actually breaks

This is where "no labels" stops being a nice sentence and starts being a genuine problem: without a ground-truth answer to check against, most of what can silently go wrong here doesn't throw an error — it just quietly produces a plausible-looking, wrong result. Representation collapse

Every joint-embedding method above has one universally available, trivially perfect shortcut: make the encoder output the exact same constant vector for every input, regardless of content. Positive pairs? Trivially identical — perfect similarity. That's not a fluke a naive loss function can tell apart from actually-good representations for free — you have to build in a mechanism that specifically forbids it. Representation collapse is this failure mode, and it's the central design problem in this whole family of methods.

Contrastive methods like SimCLR and MoCo avoid it directly: negative pairs are explicitly pushed apart, so the collapsed constant-output solution now scores as badly as possible on every negative term in the loss — collapsing destroys the part of the loss pushing negatives apart just as much as it satisfies the part pulling positives together, so there's no net incentive to take the shortcut.

BYOL and SimSiam remove negative pairs entirely and still, empirically, don't collapse, which is the surprising result. Both rely on an asymmetry between the two branches instead. In BYOL, one branch — the online network — is trained normally by gradient descent and ends with an extra predictor layer that tries to predict the other branch's output; the other branch — the target network — never receives a gradient at all, since its weights are only ever updated as a slow exponential moving average of the online network's weights, with gradients explicitly stopped from flowing into it (a stop-gradient operation). SimSiam simplifies this further, drops the momentum-averaged target network altogether, and shows that a predictor plus stop-gradient alone is enough. The stop-gradient is the load-bearing piece in both: it breaks the trivial path where both branches could collapse to the same constant together and call it solved, because one branch is deliberately prevented from simply copying or trivially chasing the other. Worth being honest about: the full theoretical picture of exactly why stop-gradient plus a predictor reliably prevents collapse is still an active research question — the SimSiam paper offers an EM-like interpretation, but "guaranteed by a clean proof" would be an overclaim. What's solid is the empirical result, replicated many times over, that this specific asymmetric architecture reliably avoids collapse without ever needing a negative pair.

Common misconception

"Two clusters that are far apart in a t-SNE or UMAP plot are far apart in the real, high-dimensional data; clusters close together are similar." This is only reliable for which points fall into which local neighborhood — both algorithms are explicitly designed to preserve local structure, not global distances. Cluster size, the distance between two separate clusters, and even the density of points within a cluster can all be artifacts of the algorithm's hyperparameters (perplexity for t-SNE, n_neighbors for UMAP) rather than anything real in the underlying data. Treat these plots as evidence for "these points are each other's neighbors," never as evidence for exactly how different two well-separated blobs are. van der Maaten & Hinton flag this limitation directly in the original t-SNE paper — citing it is a strong answer if this comes up in an interview.

Curse of dimensionality, for distance-based methods specifically

k-means, DBSCAN, and anything built on Euclidean distance share a specific, well-documented failure as dimensionality grows: in high dimensions, the ratio between the distance to the nearest point and the distance to the farthest point tends toward 1 — everything ends up roughly equidistant from everything else, and "nearest neighbor" stops being a meaningful concept. This is exactly why clustering raw pixel-space or raw high-dimensional feature vectors directly is usually a bad idea — the standard fix is to reduce dimensionality first (PCA, or a learned embedding from an autoencoder or a self-supervised encoder) and cluster in that lower-dimensional, more meaningful space instead.

Why unsupervised evaluation is hard

Supervised learning has a built-in success metric: does the prediction match the label. Unsupervised and self-supervised methods have no such thing by construction — there's no ground truth to check a clustering or a learned embedding against, which makes "is this actually good" a genuinely harder question here than almost anywhere else in ML.

In practice you fall back on proxy metrics, and it's worth knowing their specific limits. Silhouette score measures, for each point, how much closer it is to its own cluster than to the next-nearest one, averaged over all points and ranging from -1 to 1 — useful for comparing different values of k on the same algorithm, but it still assumes roughly convex, well-separated clusters, so it can penalize a correct DBSCAN-style non-convex clustering for the wrong reasons. For self-supervised representations specifically, the standard proxy is downstream transfer performance: freeze the pretrained encoder entirely, train only a small linear classifier — a linear probe — on top of it using a labeled dataset, and report that accuracy. This is literally the evaluation protocol SimCLR, and nearly every contrastive-learning paper since, uses to report results, precisely because there's no other honest way to say "these representations are good" without eventually testing them against a real task.

07Worked example and a production use case

Tiny worked example: one InfoNCE step, by hand

Say a batch produces these (illustrative, hand-picked) cosine similarities for one image's embedding z₁ against everything else: the positive pair z₂ at 0.82, and three negatives at 0.10, -0.05, and 0.30. At a common contrastive-learning temperature of τ = 0.1, divide every similarity by τ: 8.2, 1.0, -0.5, 3.0. Exponentiate: e^8.2 ≈ 3641, e^1.0 ≈ 2.72, e^-0.5 ≈ 0.61, e^3.0 ≈ 20.1. The loss for this anchor:

$$\ell = -\log\frac{3641}{3641 + 2.72 + 0.61 + 20.1} = -\log(0.9936) \approx 0.0064$$

A tiny loss, because at this temperature the positive pair's similarity already dominates the sum overwhelmingly — the model is doing well on this anchor and gets only a small gradient nudge. Now redo the exact same embeddings with τ = 1.0 instead of 0.1: dividing by 1 leaves the raw similarities unchanged, and exponentiating gives 2.27, 1.11, 0.95, 1.35:

$$\ell = -\log\frac{2.27}{2.27 + 1.11 + 0.95 + 1.35} = -\log(0.40) \approx 0.92$$

Same embeddings, same model, wildly different loss — purely because of how much temperature sharpens or flattens the softmax. That's why temperature is a real hyperparameter worth tuning, not an afterthought: it directly controls how much of the gradient budget gets spent separating hard, nearby negatives versus negatives that were never going to be confused anyway.

Production use case: self-supervised pretraining before a small fine-tune

A common real pipeline: a large pool of unlabeled images from your own domain — product photos, medical scans, satellite imagery, whatever's abundant — and only a small, expensive-to-produce labeled set for the actual task you care about, say defect classification. Pretrain a vision encoder on the full unlabeled pool first, using a contrastive objective (SimCLR/MoCo-style) or a masked-reconstruction objective (MAE-style) — no labels touched yet. Then attach a small task-specific head to that pretrained encoder and fine-tune, or, cheaper still, train only a linear probe on the frozen features, using the small labeled set. In practice this consistently beats training the same architecture from a random initialization directly on the small labeled set alone, because the encoder already learned general visual structure — edges, textures, object parts, shape — from the much larger unlabeled pool before ever seeing a single label, and fine-tuning only has to adapt that structure to the specific task rather than learn vision from scratch on too little data.

08Interview questions

BeginnerWhat's the actual difference between unsupervised and self-supervised learning?

Both use unlabeled data, but only one produces a real training target. Unsupervised learning has no target at all — clustering and PCA just expose structure already in the data, with no loss comparing a prediction to a ground truth. Self-supervised learning manufactures a target directly from the data itself — mask a word and predict it, compare two augmented views of an image — and then trains with an ordinary supervised loss against that manufactured target.

BeginnerWalk me through the k-means algorithm.

Pick k initial centroids. Alternate two steps until nothing changes: assign every point to its nearest centroid, then recompute each centroid as the mean of the points now assigned to it. It's guaranteed to converge but only to a local optimum, so in practice you run it from several random initializations (or use k-means++ for a smarter start) and keep the best result.

IntermediateExplain what happens in the E-step and M-step of EM for a Gaussian mixture model.

The E-step fixes the current Gaussian parameters and computes, for every point, the responsibility — the posterior probability it came from each of the k Gaussians. The M-step fixes those responsibilities and re-fits each Gaussian's mean, covariance, and mixing weight, using the responsibilities as soft, per-point weights. Repeating this alternation increases the data's likelihood under the model monotonically, but only to a local optimum — k-means is the hard-assignment special case of exactly this same alternation.

IntermediateWhy does temperature matter in the InfoNCE loss?

Temperature rescales similarities before the softmax inside the loss. A small temperature sharpens the distribution, so the loss and gradient become dominated by whichever negative is currently closest to the positive — forcing the encoder to focus on separating confusable, hard negatives. A large temperature flattens the distribution, treating every negative roughly equally regardless of how close it actually is, which is a much weaker training signal.

IntermediateWhat's the practical difference between a plain autoencoder and a VAE?

A plain autoencoder's latent space has no guaranteed structure beyond whatever shape minimizes reconstruction loss, so sampling a random point from it and decoding usually produces garbage — it's not a generative model. A VAE's encoder outputs a distribution over the latent instead of a single point, and a KL-divergence term pulls that distribution toward a fixed prior (usually a standard Gaussian), which forces the latent space to be smooth and densely packed — that's what makes sampling from the prior actually generate plausible outputs. Full ELBO derivation is on 12.

DeepWhat is representation collapse, and how do BYOL/SimSiam avoid it without negative pairs?

Representation collapse is the trivial failure mode where the encoder outputs the same constant vector for every input, which vacuously satisfies "positive pairs are close" without learning anything useful. Contrastive methods prevent it with negative pairs — collapsing would also destroy the part of the loss pushing negatives apart. BYOL and SimSiam use no negatives at all; instead they rely on an asymmetry between two branches — a predictor network on one branch plus a stop-gradient (and, for BYOL, a momentum-averaged target network) on the other — that empirically prevents collapse. The exact theoretical reason this specific asymmetry works is still an active research question; what's well-established is the empirical result, replicated widely, that it reliably works.

DeepWhy is evaluating an unsupervised or self-supervised model harder than evaluating a supervised one, and what do you actually do about it?

Supervised evaluation just checks predictions against ground-truth labels. There's no equivalent ground truth for a clustering or a learned embedding, so you're reduced to proxy metrics. For clustering, something like silhouette score — but it assumes roughly convex clusters, so it can penalize a correct non-convex (DBSCAN-style) result. For self-supervised representations, the standard proxy is a linear probe: freeze the encoder, train only a small linear classifier on top using labeled data, and report that accuracy — this is the exact protocol SimCLR-style papers use to claim their representations are actually useful.
