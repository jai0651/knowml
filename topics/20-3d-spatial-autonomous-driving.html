3D, Spatial AI & Autonomous Driving

Every sensor on a self-driving car gives you an incomplete, differently-shaped view of the world — cameras throw away depth, LiDAR throws away color and density, radar throws away almost everything except velocity. This page is about the geometry and the learned representations that stitch those partial views into one 3D picture accurate enough to bet a car's braking decision on.

Know conceptually ~24 min · prerequisites: CNNs & detection basics (05), attention & transformers (08)
TL;DR

Camera geometry (intrinsics, extrinsics, epipolar constraints) is the mathematical glue that lets you take pixels from several differently-positioned cameras and project them into one shared 3D — or, more commonly, bird's-eye-view (BEV) — coordinate frame; once everything lives in that shared frame, detection, tracking, prediction and planning all become standard learned problems on top of it instead of per-camera special cases. Modern perception stacks fuse camera, LiDAR, and radar because each sensor fails differently — cameras are cheap and semantically rich but bad at depth and useless in the dark, LiDAR gives precise 3D geometry but is expensive and sparse at range, radar is the only one that measures velocity directly via Doppler but at very coarse resolution — and BEV transformers (BEVFormer) and occupancy networks (TPVFormer) are the current best answer for fusing them into one representation instead of stapling together separate per-sensor detectors. Once you have that fused representation, trajectory prediction forecasts where every other agent is going next, and motion planning turns that into a safe, comfortable trajectory for your own car — and almost every deployed system keeps these as separate, debuggable stages rather than one end-to-end network, precisely because "why did the car do that" needs a debuggable answer, not just a working one. One sentence for an interview: autonomous driving is a real-time 3D perception problem wearing a robotics costume, and its central engineering tension is fusing complementary, imperfect sensors into one consistent world model fast enough, and reliably enough, to plan around safely.

01Where this sits in the timeline

Self-driving research predates deep learning by decades, and the geometric core of this page — how a camera's pixels relate to real-world 3D points — is unchanged 19th-century projective geometry, not something deep learning invented. What changed, in three overlapping waves, was how much of the pipeline above that geometry got replaced by learned components.

Before (2000s – early 2010s)

Structure-from-motion and classical stereo matching for depth; hand-engineered feature detectors (SIFT-style) for tracking; Kalman-filter-based multi-object tracking; rule-based planners built from explicit if-this-then-that logic. Rigorous and interpretable, but each hand-engineered piece degrades in exactly the conditions real driving throws at it — glare, rain, an object shape nobody hand-coded a rule for.

→
Innovation (2016 – 2022)

Deep learning took over 3D object detection first — PointPillars (2018) made LiDAR detection fast and end-to-end learnable by encoding points into a pseudo-image a normal 2D CNN could process. SLAM went tightly-coupled visual-inertial (ORB-SLAM3, 2020), fusing camera and IMU data in one optimization instead of bolting them together loosely. Then multi-camera transformers arrived: BEVFormer (2022) used learned cross-attention to lift several camera views directly into one shared bird's-eye-view grid, replacing a pipeline of "detect per-camera, then fuse detections" with "fuse features first, detect once."

→
After (2023 – 2026)

Occupancy networks (TPVFormer and successors) generalize past bounding boxes entirely, predicting free space and semantics per voxel so an oddly-shaped obstacle a detector was never trained to name still shows up as "occupied." NeRF and Gaussian splatting (06) get reused to reconstruct real driving logs photorealistically for closed-loop simulation. The frontier argument is modular-but-learned versus end-to-end: whether perception, prediction and planning stay separate, independently-verifiable learned stages, or collapse into one jointly-trained network — see section 05 for why this isn't settled yet.

02The intuition, before any equation

No single sensor tells you what's actually out there. The whole field exists because you have to combine several incomplete answers into one you can trust.

A camera image is a 2D projection of the world — by the time light hits the sensor, depth is already gone. Two objects at wildly different distances can land on the exact same pixel if one is behind the other, and a monocular camera alone genuinely cannot tell you which is which without additional assumptions or a second view to triangulate against. LiDAR fixes exactly that: it fires laser pulses and times their return, giving precise, direct 3D range measurements — but a rotating LiDAR unit only samples a sparse, uneven point cloud (dense up close, sparse at range, and it says nothing at all about an object's color or fine texture), and it's the most expensive sensor in the stack. Radar solves a different problem: its Doppler shift measures velocity directly, instantly, which neither cameras nor LiDAR do without inferring it across multiple frames — but radar's spatial resolution is coarse enough that it can't reliably tell you an object's exact shape or, sometimes, whether two nearby objects are one object or two.

Every technique on this page is really answering one of two questions. First: how do you turn what one sensor sees into a 3D (or bird's-eye-view) fact about the world — that's camera geometry, stereo depth, and LiDAR point-cloud processing. Second: how do you combine facts from sensors that disagree, measure different things, and update at different rates into one coherent picture you can actually plan around — that's sensor fusion, BEV representations, and occupancy networks. Get the first question right and you have accurate individual sensor readings that still don't agree with each other. Get the second right and disagreement between sensors becomes a source of robustness — each one covers the others' blind spots — instead of a source of confusion.

03The stack, piece by piece

A modern autonomous driving stack is a pipeline, much like the robotics pipeline in 21, but with 3D geometry doing more of the early work: raw sensors feed geometric and learned perception, perception output gets fused into one shared representation, that representation feeds prediction and planning, and planning's output becomes control commands.

Sensors → shared 3D representation → plan — one pipeline, several sensor streams
Three sensor streams, running at different rates and with different failure modes, get lifted into one shared bird's-eye-view or occupancy representation before anything downstream ever runs. Detection and tracking, prediction, and planning are almost always kept as separate stages — see section 05 for why.

Camera geometry: intrinsics, extrinsics, and why multi-camera needs both

A camera's intrinsic matrix $K$ describes properties fixed by the lens and sensor itself — focal length and the principal point (where the optical axis actually hits the sensor) — and maps a 3D point already expressed in the camera's own coordinate frame down to a 2D pixel. The extrinsic transform, a rotation $R$ and translation $t$, describes where that camera physically sits and points relative to the vehicle (or the world) — it converts a point from world coordinates into that specific camera's coordinate frame, before the intrinsics take over and project it to a pixel. A single camera only ever needs its own intrinsics and extrinsics. A multi-camera rig needs every camera's extrinsics relative to one shared reference frame (typically the vehicle's own center), because that shared frame is precisely what lets you take detections or features from six differently-mounted cameras and place them all correctly relative to each other instead of six disconnected pixel grids.

Epipolar geometry, stereo, and depth

Given two cameras with a known, fixed offset (a stereo rig), the same real-world point appears in both images, and epipolar geometry says something powerful: once you know the two cameras' relative pose, a point's location in the first image constrains its possible location in the second image to a single line — the epipolar line — instead of the whole 2D image. That collapses stereo matching from a 2D search problem into a 1D one, which is the difference between "compute this on every frame in real time" and "don't." Once a correspondence is found, depth falls out directly from the geometry: farther points shift less between the two images (smaller disparity), nearer points shift more (larger disparity), and disparity is inversely proportional to depth for a fixed camera baseline.

SLAM and visual-inertial odometry

Simultaneous localization and mapping is a genuine chicken-and-egg problem: to know where you are, it helps to already have a map; to build a map, it helps to already know where you are. SLAM solves both at once by jointly estimating the camera's trajectory and a map of tracked landmarks, continuously refining both as new observations arrive. Visual-inertial SLAM (ORB-SLAM3 is the well-known open-source reference implementation) tightly couples camera tracking with an IMU's acceleration and angular velocity readings — the IMU fills in the fast, high-frequency motion estimate between camera frames and stays useful through the camera's worst moments (motion blur, brief occlusion, a tunnel), while the camera corrects the IMU's slow drift whenever it can see stable landmarks again.

3D object detection: camera-based and LiDAR-based

LiDAR-based detection has to solve an awkward data-structure problem first: a point cloud is sparse, unordered, and has a wildly uneven density (dense close to the sensor, sparse far away) — nothing a standard CNN, built for dense, regular pixel grids, can consume directly. PointPillars' answer is to discretize the ground plane into a grid of vertical columns ("pillars"), run a small shared network over the points inside each pillar to produce one feature vector per pillar, and arrange those vectors into a 2D pseudo-image that an entirely ordinary 2D CNN detector can then process — turning an irregular 3D problem into a regular 2D one without needing 3D convolutions at all, which is exactly why it runs fast enough for a moving vehicle. Camera-based 3D detection has the harder job of estimating depth that was never directly measured, either per-camera (monocular depth estimation, generally less accurate) or jointly across camera views the way the BEV methods below do it, which is a large part of why multi-camera BEV approaches displaced monocular 3D detection as the camera-only default.

BEV representations: one shared top-down frame

Bird's-eye-view (BEV) is a top-down 2D grid over the ground plane around the vehicle, and it's the natural shared coordinate frame for driving specifically, because planning a route and reasoning about lanes and other vehicles is inherently a top-down problem — nobody plans a driving maneuver by thinking in raw camera-pixel coordinates. BEVFormer builds this grid directly with attention: a fixed grid of learned BEV queries, one per top-down grid cell, uses spatial cross-attention to pull in the relevant image features from whichever camera views actually see that patch of ground, and temporal self-attention to fuse the current BEV grid with the previous timestep's grid — which noticeably helps estimate a moving object's velocity and recover objects briefly occluded a frame ago, since the model isn't restarting perception from zero every single frame.

Occupancy networks: past the bounding box

A bounding box assumes the world is made of a fixed, known set of object categories with roughly box-shaped extents — which is a bad assumption for a fallen ladder, a spilled load of cargo, or any obstacle shape a detector's training set never named. Occupancy networks sidestep that by predicting, for every cell in a 3D (or, to save compute, a compressed) grid around the vehicle, whether it's occupied and, if so, by roughly what kind of thing — turning "list the objects" into "describe free space directly," which degrades far more gracefully on anything unusual. TPVFormer's specific trick for making this tractable is representing the volume as three orthogonal 2D planes (top-down, plus the two perpendicular side views) instead of one dense 3D voxel grid, recovering the full 3D structure by combining the three planes — a meaningfully cheaper representation than predicting every voxel in a full 3D grid directly.

NeRF, Gaussian splatting, and scene reconstruction

06 covers how NeRF and Gaussian splatting work as general 3D scene representations. The driving-specific use is reconstruction for simulation: take a real recorded driving log, reconstruct the actual scene the car drove through as a NeRF or a set of Gaussians, and then re-render it from camera viewpoints that differ from where the car originally drove, or with other actors inserted or removed — much closer to testing against reality than a purely synthetic simulator, since the environment itself was reconstructed from a real drive rather than hand-modeled.

Scene graphs and spatial reasoning

A scene graph represents the world as nodes (vehicles, pedestrians, lanes, traffic signals) connected by labeled relational edges ("in front of," "yielding to," "occupies this lane") instead of a flat, unstructured list of detections. That structure is what makes higher-level spatial queries answerable directly — "which vehicles are relevant to my current lane change" is a graph query over relationships, not a re-derivation from raw geometry every time it's asked.

Sensor fusion: camera, LiDAR, and radar together

Fusion can happen early (combine near-raw sensor data before much processing — hardest to get right, but no information is thrown away before combination), late (run separate full detectors per sensor and combine only their final outputs — simplest to build and debug, but each detector may have already discarded a cue only visible when signals are considered jointly), or somewhere in between (fuse intermediate features — BEVFormer-style camera fusion, extended to also ingest LiDAR or radar features into the same shared grid, is the currently favored middle ground). Camera, LiDAR, and radar are specifically chosen as a complementary trio, not simply "more sensors are better": camera supplies rich semantic detail (what is this, what color, what does the sign say) that LiDAR and radar structurally cannot; LiDAR supplies precise geometry that monocular camera depth estimation cannot match; radar supplies direct, instantaneous velocity and keeps functioning through fog, rain and glare that badly degrade both cameras and (to a lesser extent) LiDAR.

Trajectory prediction and motion planning

Trajectory prediction takes each tracked agent's recent motion history plus map context (lanes, right-of-way, traffic controls) and forecasts several plausible future paths, because "what will that pedestrian do" is inherently multimodal — they might cross, might not, and a model that averages both possibilities into one path is exactly as wrong as the mode-averaging problem in 21's behavioral-cloning discussion. MotionNet is a clean illustration of one way to structure this: it takes BEV maps as input and predicts, per grid cell, both a category and a short-horizon motion vector, unifying detection and near-term prediction inside one shared BEV representation rather than treating them as two disconnected models. Motion planning then takes the ego vehicle's own goal plus every predicted agent trajectory and searches for a trajectory that's safe (doesn't intersect anyone else's likely future path), comfortable (bounded acceleration and jerk), and legal (obeys lane and traffic-control constraints) — conceptually the same constrained trajectory-optimization idea as MPC in 21, just planning one long route instead of one short re-planned step, and re-run continuously as new predictions arrive.

End-to-end autonomous driving, and world models for simulation

End-to-end driving trains one network to map sensor inputs directly to a planned trajectory or even direct control outputs, optimized jointly against one end task loss instead of stage-by-stage intermediate losses — in principle able to discover that, say, a slightly different detection threshold would have produced a much better downstream plan, a joint optimization the modular pipeline's separately-trained stages can't see. The tradeoffs of that approach, and why the industry hasn't broadly converged on it for safety-critical deployment yet, are covered in section 05. World models (22) increasingly get used here as fast, learned simulators: instead of only testing planning against a fixed replay log or a hand-built physics simulator, roll candidate plans forward inside a learned model of how the scene evolves, cheaply and safely, before ever testing them in the real world or even a full simulator.

Closed-loop evaluation for driving

The same open-loop-versus-closed-loop distinction from 21 applies here with even higher stakes. Open-loop evaluation replays a fixed, previously recorded log and checks whether the planner's outputs match what actually happened — cheap, fast, fully reproducible, but blind to compounding error, because the ego vehicle's own hypothetical actions never get to change what the scenario does next, so a bad early decision never gets the chance to cascade into a worse one the way it would in reality. Closed-loop evaluation actually lets the planner's decisions affect the simulated scenario going forward, in a simulator (increasingly, a NeRF/Gaussian-splatting reconstruction of a real log, or a learned world model rather than a hand-authored simulator), and is the only way to catch the failures that only show up once the car has already made one small mistake.

04The math, term by term

Two equations carry almost all of the geometry on this page: the camera projection equation, and the epipolar constraint.

$$\lambda \begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{bmatrix} = K \big[R \mid t\big] \begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\\1\end{bmatrix}$$
  • X, Y, Z a point's coordinates in the world (or vehicle) frame.
  • R, t the camera's extrinsics — a rotation and translation converting that world-frame point into the camera's own coordinate frame.
  • K the camera's intrinsic matrix — focal length and principal point — projecting the camera-frame 3D point down onto the 2D image plane.
  • u, v the resulting pixel coordinates, up to the scale factor $\lambda$ (the point's depth, which the projection alone throws away — the entire reason depth estimation is a separate, hard problem).

The epipolar constraint, for two calibrated cameras viewing the same point:

$$x'^{\top} F x = 0$$
  • x, x' the same real-world point's pixel coordinates in the first and second camera respectively.
  • F the fundamental matrix — encodes the two cameras' relative geometry (their relative $R, t$ plus intrinsics).
  • Given $x$ and $F$, this equation defines a single line in the second image — the epipolar line — that $x'$ is guaranteed to lie on. That's the entire reason stereo matching only needs a 1D search along a line rather than a full 2D image search.

05Why BEV/occupancy, and why modular over end-to-end

Fusing raw per-camera detections after the fact is the obvious first design, and it's a worse one: each camera's detector has already thrown away information by the time it commits to a final box, so if two cameras jointly saw a partially-occluded object, but neither one alone saw enough to be confident, late fusion never gets the chance to combine their partial evidence — the information needed to resolve it was already discarded before fusion ever ran. Lifting features into a shared BEV grid before detecting anything keeps that partial evidence alive long enough to be combined, which is the direct reason BEVFormer-style architectures displaced "detect per camera, then merge boxes" as the default. Occupancy networks push the same logic further: a bounding-box vocabulary is a closed set of categories decided at training time, and the real world reliably produces obstacles outside that set; predicting occupancy directly, instead of committing to "this is definitely a car, definitely this size," degrades gracefully on exactly the long-tail shapes a fixed category list can't name.

The modular-versus-end-to-end question doesn't have a settled answer, and treating it as settled is a tell that someone hasn't thought about the actual tradeoff. A modular pipeline — perception, then prediction, then planning, as separate stages with separate losses — is independently testable and debuggable at every stage: if the car braked unnecessarily, you can inspect whether perception mis-detected something, prediction mis-forecast a trajectory, or planning made a bad call given otherwise-correct inputs, and validate each stage against its own held-out data. An end-to-end network optimized on one final task loss can in principle find a jointly better solution across all three stages at once — a slightly different detection behavior that happens to produce much better downstream plans — but that joint optimum arrives with no clean way to say why the network did what it did, which is precisely the property regulators, safety cases, and post-incident investigations need from a system this safety-critical. That single tension — global optimality versus stage-by-stage interpretability — is the actual reason modular pipelines remain the deployed standard while end-to-end approaches stay a very active, not-yet-dominant research direction.

What actually shows up in production

  • Sensor miscalibration — extrinsics drift slightly out of alignment over time (a bump, temperature change, a minor collision) and every downstream fusion step silently trusts a wrong camera-to-vehicle transform, producing subtly wrong 3D positions that look plausible.
  • Long-tail object shapes that a bounding-box detector's training categories never covered — exactly what occupancy networks exist to catch, and exactly where pure bounding-box pipelines fail quietly instead of loudly.
  • Radar/camera disagreement in edge cases — a stationary large metal object can produce a weak or ambiguous radar return, and if the fusion logic over-trusts radar's usually-reliable velocity signal, a real stationary hazard can be under-weighted.
  • Compounding error across stages — prediction errors get fed straight into planning as if they were ground truth, so an overconfident, wrong trajectory forecast for another vehicle can produce a planning decision that's optimal for the wrong predicted future.
Common misconception

"More sensors is strictly more robust." Not automatically — fusion has to correctly handle sensors that disagree, and a fusion system with a bad prior (over-trusting one modality, or a subtly miscalibrated extrinsic transform) can produce a worse combined estimate than the single best sensor would have given alone. Sensor diversity only helps if the fusion logic actually knows how much to trust each sensor under the current conditions, which is a real design problem, not something that comes for free just because more data streams exist.

2026 status

BEV transformers and occupancy networks are the deployed standard for perception on modern multi-camera (and camera+LiDAR) stacks. Modular pipelines — separate perception, prediction, and planning stages — remain the deployed default for safety-critical systems; end-to-end driving is an active, fast-moving research area with promising results on benchmarks, but has not broadly displaced modular pipelines in safety-certified production systems, and claims that it has should be treated skeptically absent a specific, verifiable source.

07Worked example and a production use case

Illustrative worked example: stereo depth from disparity

Say a stereo rig has a baseline (distance between the two cameras) of b = 0.5 m and a focal length of f = 700 pixels — illustrative, round numbers, not a real system's spec sheet. A point produces a disparity (how far it shifts between the left and right image) of d = 35 pixels. Depth falls directly out of the geometry: depth = f · b / d = 700 × 0.5 / 35 = 10 m. Notice the shape of that relationship: a nearby object with a large disparity of, say, 175 pixels would come out to 700 × 0.5 / 175 ≈ 2 m, while a far object with a small disparity of 7 pixels gives 700 × 0.5 / 7 = 50 m — small disparities correspond to large distances, which is exactly why stereo depth estimation gets noisy and unreliable at long range: a 1-pixel error in a 7-pixel disparity measurement is a much larger relative error than a 1-pixel error in a 175-pixel one.

Production use case: multi-camera BEV perception feeding prediction and planning

A representative modern stack: six to eight cameras around the vehicle (sometimes paired with LiDAR) feed a BEV transformer that lifts every camera's features into one shared top-down grid, fusing across cameras and across the last few timesteps in the same step. Detection and tracking run on top of that fused BEV grid, producing tracked objects with position, velocity, and a class. A trajectory predictor takes those tracks plus map context and forecasts several plausible short-horizon futures per agent. A planner searches over candidate ego trajectories against all of those predictions plus map and traffic-rule constraints, and the winning trajectory gets hasnded to a classical trajectory-tracking controller (21) to execute. Public autonomous-driving research benchmarks like nuScenes and the Waymo Open Dataset exist specifically so different groups' perception and prediction stacks can be compared against the same real, sensor-synchronized driving logs rather than each group's own private data.

08Interview questions

BeginnerWhy do self-driving cars need multiple sensor types instead of just one really good camera?

Because each sensor fails differently. Cameras are cheap and semantically rich but only give a 2D projection — depth has to be inferred, not measured, and they degrade badly in low light or glare. LiDAR measures 3D geometry directly and precisely, but it's expensive, sparse at range, and tells you nothing about color or texture. Radar measures velocity directly via Doppler shift — something neither camera nor LiDAR does without inferring it across multiple frames — but at coarse spatial resolution. No single sensor covers all three needs (rich semantics, precise geometry, direct velocity), so fusion isn't a nice-to-have, it's structurally required.

BeginnerWhat's the difference between a camera's intrinsics and extrinsics?

Intrinsics describe properties fixed by the camera itself — focal length and principal point — and map a 3D point already in the camera's own coordinate frame to a 2D pixel. Extrinsics describe where the camera is physically mounted and how it's oriented relative to some external reference frame — a rotation and translation converting a point from that external frame into the camera's frame, before intrinsics take over. A multi-camera rig needs every camera's extrinsics relative to one shared frame so their detections can be placed consistently relative to each other.

IntermediateHow does BEVFormer turn several camera images into one shared representation?

It defines a fixed grid of learned query vectors, one per cell of a top-down bird's-eye-view grid. Each query uses spatial cross-attention to pull in features from whichever camera views actually observe that patch of ground, using known camera geometry to know where to look. It also uses temporal self-attention against the previous timestep's BEV grid, which helps estimate velocity and recover briefly-occluded objects, since the model isn't reconstructing the scene from scratch every single frame. The result is one shared top-down feature grid that detection, tracking, and everything downstream can consume, instead of per-camera detections that have to be merged after the fact.

IntermediateWhy would you use an occupancy network instead of a standard 3D object detector?

A bounding-box detector assumes a fixed, closed set of object categories decided at training time, and real driving reliably produces obstacles that don't fit any of them — a fallen ladder, spilled cargo, debris. An occupancy network predicts, per grid cell, whether space is occupied and roughly what kind of thing occupies it, which degrades gracefully on unusual shapes instead of failing to detect them at all, because it never had to commit to a specific named category to register that the space is occupied.

DeepWhy hasn't the industry converged on end-to-end driving despite its promise of joint optimization?

End-to-end driving optimizes one network against a single end task loss, which can in principle discover jointly better solutions a modular pipeline's separately-trained stages can't see — a slightly different detection behavior that happens to produce a much better downstream plan, for instance. The cost is interpretability: when a modular pipeline makes a bad decision, you can inspect which stage — perception, prediction, or planning — was responsible, and validate each stage independently against held-out data for that specific sub-problem. An end-to-end network's decision doesn't decompose that way. For a safety-critical system that needs a debuggable, auditable answer to "why did the car do that," that loss of interpretability is a real cost, which is why modular pipelines remain the deployed default even though end-to-end methods post competitive results on research benchmarks.

DeepWhy is stereo depth estimation less reliable at long range than short range?

Depth is inversely proportional to disparity for a fixed camera baseline and focal length. At long range, disparity is already small — a handful of pixels — so a fixed amount of pixel-matching noise (say, one pixel of error) is a much larger relative error on a small disparity value than the same one-pixel error would be on the large disparity of a nearby object. That's a direct geometric consequence of the depth-disparity relationship, not a fixable calibration issue — it's why LiDAR, which measures range directly rather than through disparity, tends to be trusted more than stereo camera depth at longer distances.
