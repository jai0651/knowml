Where to Practice

Reading this site will not make you good at machine learning. Writing code that fails, and finding out why, is what does. This is where to go and do that, and what each place is actually for.

Directory ~8 min · every link checked September 2026
How to use this page

These are not interchangeable. A LeetCode-style drill site, a competition platform and a build-it-yourself course train different skills, and picking the wrong one for where you are is the usual reason practice feels unproductive.

Section 01 is the honest matching guide. The rest is what each platform is good at, and the one thing each is bad at.

01Start with what you are actually trying to fix

Four different complaints, four different answers. Find yours.

"I read it and it made sense, but I could not write it"

The most common gap, and the easiest to close. You need small, sharply-scoped problems where the answer is checkable in seconds and you cannot lean on a library call.

Deep-ML, then the Attention Lab and the Try it blocks on the topic pages here.

"I can implement things but my models are mediocre"

You have the mechanics and not the judgement: what to try first, when to stop tuning, how to read a validation curve. That is a feedback-loop problem, and competitions are a feedback loop.

Kaggle Playground competitions, then a real one.

"I use the libraries but do not know what is inside"

Nothing fixes this except building one from scratch, once. It is slower than it looks and it is worth it. You only need to do it a single time for the mental model to stick.

Karpathy's Zero to Hero, then page 29's project.

"My plain coding is slowing me down"

Worth naming separately. Plenty of people stall in ML interviews on the Python, not the ML. That is a cheaper problem to fix and you should fix it first.

Codewars or any general practice site, 20 minutes a day.

Not an ML platform. Do not conflate the two.

02Drill sites: small problems, instant feedback

The closest thing ML has to LeetCode. Best when you want reps on a specific mechanism.

⚡
Free · browser
Deep-ML
1,200+ problems across ML fundamentals, deep learning, vision and NLP, graded against test cases in the browser. The premise is that you write every line rather than calling a library, which is exactly right for building intuition.
🧩
Free · browser
Codewars
Not ML at all — general programming katas with instant feedback. Listed because slow, hesitant Python is a real and separable bottleneck, and it is worth clearing before it costs you an interview.
🔬
Free · niche
PennyLane Challenges
Quantum computing and quantum ML coding challenges with feedback. Narrow, and genuinely good if that is where you are heading. Ignore otherwise.
🔬
On this site
Attention Lab
Step through MHA, KV caching, GQA, MLA, PagedAttention and FlashAttention with every matrix computed live. Not a drill, but the fastest way to stop attention being abstract.
Why "no library calls" is the whole point

Calling nn.MultiheadAttention teaches you an API. Writing the four lines that compute it teaches you why the mask is applied before the softmax and not after.

Interviews probe the second thing. So does debugging. When a drill site forbids the shortcut, that constraint is the feature, not an inconvenience.

03Competitions: judgement under a real metric

A leaderboard is an honest verifier. It will tell you your clever idea did not work, which is information you cannot get from reading.

🏆
Free · the default
Kaggle Competitions
Submit a model, get scored immediately against a held-out set. Start with a Playground competition rather than a prize one — same loop, far less noise, and the public notebooks are the real curriculum.
📋
Free · aggregator
ML Contests
Every active ML, data science and robotics competition across 20+ platforms in one list. Useful precisely because Kaggle is not the only one, and the non-Kaggle contests are usually far less crowded.
📊
Free · report
The State of Machine Learning Competitions
An annual survey of what actually won. Read one before entering anything: it is the cheapest available correction to assuming the newest architecture is the answer.
📘
Free · short courses
Kaggle Learn
Short practical micro-courses that end in a working notebook. A reasonable on-ramp if jumping straight into a live competition feels like too big a step.
What competitions teach, and what they quietly mis-teach

They are excellent for feature work, validation discipline and knowing when to stop. Those transfer directly.

They also reward things production punishes: enormous ensembles, squeezing the fourth decimal place, and leaking information from a fixed test set that a real system would never have. The 2024 survey found gradient-boosted trees still dominating tabular winners — a useful reality check, and also a reminder that the competition setting is not the deployment setting. Page 24 covers the difference.

04Build it from scratch, once

The single highest-return thing on this page, and the one most people skip because it looks slow.

🎬
Free · video + code
Neural Networks: Zero to Hero
Andrej Karpathy builds backprop, then a language model, then GPT, in order, typing every line. If you do one thing on this page, do this. It is the reference for how this material should be taught.
🤗
Free · courses
Hugging Face Learn
Practical courses on NLP, diffusion, RL and agents, built around the libraries you will actually use in a job. Complements Karpathy: he shows the inside, this shows the tooling.
🏋️
Free · curriculum
ARENA
A structured implementation curriculum covering transformers, interpretability and RL, with exercises and solutions. Considerably more demanding than a course, and closer to how research engineers are actually trained.
📄
Free · index
Papers with Code
Papers paired with implementations and leaderboards. The place to go when you want to reimplement something and check your numbers against a reference.

05What to practise on this site

Every page here has something to do, not just something to read. Collected in one place.

  • Try it blocks. Runnable snippets on the topic pages, each one short enough to read in place and verified to produce the output its caption claims. Copy them and change a number — that is where the learning is.
  • Build this projects. Every full topic page ends with one, scoped to an afternoon and to hardware you already own. Page 31's latency wall and page 16's feedback-loop simulation are the two that change how people think.
  • The Attention Lab. Seven attention mechanisms, every matrix computed live, with FlashAttention checking itself against the plain-softmax result.
  • The Review page. 247 interview questions on a spaced-repetition schedule. Answer before revealing, and rate honestly.
A sequence that works

Read a page. Run its Try it blocks and break one deliberately. Do the Build this project. Drill that page's questions in the Review page a week later.

That is four passes over the same material, spaced, with production required at each step. It beats reading four different explanations by a wide margin, and it is slower in a way that is the entire point.
