Suppose your company has asked you to put an assistant in front of its support documentation. A customer types a question, the assistant finds the right help-centre articles and writes an answer from them. The first thing anyone asks is "which model should we use?", and there are a few hundred to pick from. That's the question this chapter is built around, and we'll keep coming back to this one support assistant as we narrow the field.

Most people answer by opening a leaderboard and taking whatever sits at the top. That works badly, because a leaderboard ranks general ability averaged over tasks you don't run, and your assistant has constraints that a ranking never sees. Can your customers' tickets be sent to someone else's servers? How long can a customer wait for the first word of a reply? How much money can each answer cost? We'll ask those questions first, in an order that shrinks the field quickly, and only then look at scores. After that we'll walk through three tiers of models (closed models behind an API, open-weight models you can download, and small models that fit on one machine), then the licences that decide what you're allowed to do with them, the embedding models that find the right articles, and the arithmetic of what it all costs.

One warning before any numbers. This page was assembled on 6 September 2026, and models are released nearly every week, with leaderboard positions shifting within days of each release. So check the date on any comparison before you quote it, and rebuild your shortlist from a live leaderboard, including when you're checking this page. What stays true over time is the structure: what each benchmark measures, how licences differ, and how cost is computed. Those are the parts to remember, and the tables are just a starting shortlist.

01The order to ask the questions in

Two hundred models is too many to compare, so the first job is to cut the field down with questions that have yes-or-no answers.

Start with where the model will run, since that cut is the cheapest. A closed model is one you reach through a company's API, so your text travels to their servers and comes back as a reply. An open-weight model is one whose weights (the learned numbers that make up the model) you can download and run on hardware you control. For the support assistant, tickets contain customer names and account details, so suppose your security team says that data can't leave your network. That one answer removes every closed model at once, and you're choosing among open-weight models only. Ask this first, because it eliminates the most.

Second comes the licence you can accept, since open weights aren't one thing. Some are released under Apache 2.0, some carry revenue thresholds, and some forbid commercial use outright. We'll take that apart in section 06. Third is the latency budget, meaning how long a user waits. Two numbers matter: the time to first token (how long until the first piece of the reply appears) and the tokens per second after that. A token is a word or a piece of a word, the unit models read and write. Both numbers depend on the serving software as much as on the model, and page 31 explains the mechanism. Here we only use them as a filter.

Fourth is how much context you need. A context window is the amount of text the model can look at in one request, and the advertised size and the size at which the model still behaves well are different numbers. Long context also has a price, because the model has to read all of it before it replies (that reading step is called prefill) and has to keep notes on it in memory for the whole reply. For the assistant you'd send a question plus a handful of retrieved articles, far less than a million tokens, so this question barely narrows anything.

Only now do we get to quality, and the right question isn't "which model is best?" It's "what is the smallest model that clears my quality bar, measured on my own data?" Ranked lists answer something almost nobody is asking. They rank general capability, averaged over tasks you don't run, on inputs that look nothing like yours. Your question is narrower: which model is good enough, inside my latency budget, at a price I can pay, under a licence my lawyer will sign. Best and good enough are often different models, and the second is probably several times cheaper. Cost comes last, because its answer means little until the other questions have shrunk the list to about three candidates. Next we'll cover how to read the public scores that help you pick those three.

02Reading the benchmark board

Public scores are how you choose which models deserve a place in your own test, so you need to know what each column measures and how it can mislead.

What each one tests

A benchmark is a fixed set of questions with known answers, run against many models so their scores can be compared. When the best models all score close to the maximum, we say the benchmark is saturated, and it stops telling them apart. These are the ones you'll see most often on leaderboards.

  • MMLU-Pro is ten-option multiple choice across academic subjects, a harder rebuild of the original MMLU. It measures recall plus light reasoning. Strong models cluster near the top of it, so it separates them poorly.
  • GPQA Diamond is a small set of graduate-level science questions, written so that a non-expert with a search engine still fails. It's a genuine reasoning test, but the set is small enough that a handful of items swings the score.
  • AIME is competition maths, with fifteen problems per contest. One problem is worth about 6.7 points, so run-to-run variance is large and single-run numbers deserve suspicion.
  • SWE-bench Verified uses real GitHub issues from real repositories, and humans have checked they're solvable. It has to produce a patch that passes the project's tests.
  • Terminal-Bench gives the model end-to-end tasks in a real shell. It measures the whole loop of planning, running commands, reading the output and recovering from failure.
  • LiveCodeBench collects programming problems published after each model's training cutoff, a design that makes it resistant to contamination (we'll define that shortly).
  • Humanity's Last Exam (HLE) was deliberately built to stay unsaturated. Its scores are low, and that leaves headroom to tell models apart.
  • LMArena Elo comes from humans voting blind between two responses, with the votes turned into a rating the way chess players are rated. It captures things static benchmarks miss, and it rewards formatting and tone as well as correctness.

The four traps

Knowing what a score measures isn't enough, because four things can distort it even when the benchmark is a good one. First, a modern model isn't one point on the chart. The same weights, run at low, high and maximum reasoning effort (how many tokens the model spends thinking before it answers) produce different scores, different latencies and different prices. Artificial Analysis, the site we'll use for the tables below, lists these as separate rows for exactly this reason. Comparing one vendor's maximum setting against another's default isn't a comparison.

Second, there's a trap on the agent-style tests like SWE-bench and Terminal-Bench. There the model runs inside a scaffold, the code around it that supplies tools, retries failed steps, manages the context and formats file edits. That scaffold does a lot of the work. Changes to the retry policy, the tool definitions, the context handling or the edit format can move a score by double digits with the weights unchanged, so a number whose scaffold isn't described can't be reproduced.

Third is contamination, which means the test questions leaked into the training data. Public benchmarks get copied around the web, and web text is what models train on, so a high score on a five-year-old benchmark may mean the model memorised the answers. That's why benchmarks with rolling, post-cutoff problems exist. Fourth, vendor-published tables are run by the vendor, on their scaffold, with their prompt. Independent re-runs routinely come in lower, so prefer a third-party harness when the decision matters.

Put those four together and the answer to "which number should decide it?" is none of them. For the support assistant, you'd collect fifty prompts drawn from real tickets, write a grader you trust, and run every candidate against them. A public benchmark tells you which models belong in that bake-off, and your fifty prompts tell you which one to ship. Teams that skip the second step spend the next quarter debugging a model that ranked well and doesn't fit. The Build section after the summary walks through that project, probably the most useful thing on this page. First we'll look at who's in the bake-off, starting at the top.

03The frontier tier

If your data is allowed to leave the building, the first candidates for the support assistant are the closed frontier models.

These models sit behind an API. You get the strongest general capability available, and you can't inspect, host or pin the weights, so you're trusting the vendor's behaviour from one week to the next. 04The open-weight tier

Back to our assistant with the data that can't leave your network: these are the models you can choose from, because you can download, host, quantise, fine-tune and pin the weights.

(To quantise a model is to store its weights with fewer bits each so it takes less memory, which we'll lean on in the next section.) Most teams with a data-residency requirement end up in this tier, and the ecosystem's centre of gravity has moved here too. Today the strongest open models sit within a few points of the closed frontier on published aggregates, and the gap that remains is narrower than the gap between a good prompt and a bad one.

Look at the parameter column, because it holds two numbers for some models and one for others, and the pair is what changes your hardware bill. Many of these are mixture-of-experts (MoE) models. Inside such a model, each layer holds many separate sub-networks called experts, and a small router picks a few of them for every token. So "2.8T / 104B MoE" means 2.8 trillion parameters in total, of which 104 billion are used for any one token.

Why does that split matter for cost? Because the router can pick any expert for the next token, every expert has to be loaded in the memory of your GPUs (VRAM) at all times. Total parameters therefore set the VRAM you need, and so the size of the rack. But only the chosen experts do any multiplication for a given token, so active parameters set how much arithmetic each token costs, and with it the decoding speed. Kimi K3 is the extreme case. It decodes at roughly the cost of a 104B dense model and needs the memory of a 2.8T one. That's a good deal if you own a rack and out of reach if you own a workstation. The diagram below draws one token's route through Kimi K3.

The grid is drawn to scale: 112 cells standing for 896 experts at 8 each, so the two lit cells are 16 experts firing. Everything in the grid occupies memory on every pass; only the lit part multiplies anything. Note that the expert ratio and the parameter ratio are not the same number — 16 of 896 experts is 56× sparsity, while 104B of 2.8T params is about 27×, because attention and the shared layers run for every token regardless of routing. The total tells you what hardware you must own; the active count tells you how fast it runs once you do.

Next, the licence column, because those entries don't all mean the same thing. GLM-5.3-Flash and the Qwen3.8 line ship under MIT and Apache 2.0, and those impose almost nothing. Kimi K3 ships under a bespoke licence with revenue thresholds. Section 06 covers what that means in practice.

One structural change is also visible in the modality column. Open-weight releases are now natively multimodal by default and no longer a later variant, so a model that reads images arrives in the first release. Kimi K3 carries its own vision encoder, and the Qwen3.8 and GLM-5.3-Flash lines accept images as a matter of course. All of these still assume a rack, though, and plenty of teams have one machine, which brings us to the small end.

05The tier that runs on your own machine

Suppose the assistant has to run on a single workstation, or a phone app has to answer offline. Small models are a separate design target, tuned for a fixed memory budget and a fixed power envelope.

They aren't scaled-down frontier models, and what decides whether one is usable is arithmetic you can do yourself. Page 30 derives it. Weights in memory take roughly the number of parameters times the bytes each parameter takes, so a 27B model at 4-bit (half a byte per parameter) needs about 14 GB before the KV cache, the extra memory the model uses to remember the text it has already read. That number decides what fits, and a benchmark can't.

That arithmetic fills the "Fits on" column. One entry needs explaining, Gemma 4's effective parameters. That E-series reports an effective count lower than the raw one because part of the network isn't resident for each token, so read the effective count as the memory figure that matters.

Reaching for the largest model that fits is the most common mistake at this tier. A smaller model with retrieval probably does better, because for the support assistant the facts live in the help-centre articles and the model only has to read them well. Another option is a smaller model plus one fine-tune on your task, and page 29 covers when that fine-tune is worth it.

The smallest entries have a different job again. A sub-1B model isn't a chat model, and judging it as one misses what it's for. Its jobs are narrow: a draft model for speculative decoding, an intent classifier, a router in front of a larger model, or structured extraction with a fixed schema. For our assistant, a 0.6B model could read each incoming ticket and decide whether it needs the big model at all. Each of those is a real production role. Whichever tier you land in, though, one more document decides whether you may ship it.

06Licences: the section everyone skips

A model that passes your eval can still be one you aren't allowed to use. This is the most common way a model choice goes wrong six months later, and twenty minutes of reading avoids it.

A licence is the document that says what you may do with the weights. Open-weight licences fall into four buckets, and the differences aren't cosmetic.

  • Permissive and standard licences are Apache 2.0 or MIT. They allow commercial use, modification and redistribution with no thresholds. The Qwen3.8 line, Gemma 4, Granite 4.2 and GLM-5.3-Flash sit here. Gemma moved to Apache 2.0 at version 4, replacing the custom terms earlier Gemma releases carried.
  • Revenue-tiered licences are free until your business crosses a threshold, and then a separate agreement or an attribution requirement kicks in. Kimi K3's licence requires an agreement to run it as a paid model-as-a-service above roughly $20M annual revenue from that use.
  • Research and non-commercial licences let you evaluate a model but not ship it. They're common in speech and audio, where several strong models are gated this way.
  • Closed models come with API access under terms of service. There are no weights, no pinning and no offline use.

You'll often hear "open weights" and "open source" used as if they meant the same thing, and they don't. Open weights means the parameter file can be downloaded. Open source, in the sense the term has carried for twenty-five years, implies a licence with no field-of-use restrictions and, under the OSI's AI definition, meaningful information about the training data. Most models called open source in press coverage are open weights under a custom licence, so read the licence file and ignore the headline. Ask a narrow question: does this licence permit the specific thing my company is about to do?

Two practical checks follow before you commit. Confirm that the licence covers distillation if you plan to train a smaller model on the big model's outputs, because several licences restrict this. And confirm what happens to your fine-tuned derivative, because a derivative usually inherits the base licence. With a model and a licence settled, we can go back to the part of the assistant that decides which articles the model ever sees.

07Embeddings and rerankers

Our assistant writes answers from retrieved articles, so if the retrieval step misses the right article, no generator can recover. Retrieval quality is usually the ceiling on a RAG system, and the embedding model sets that ceiling.

An embedding model maps text to a vector, a list of numbers, so that similar meanings land close together. You embed every help-centre article once, embed the customer's question when it arrives, and take the articles whose vectors are nearest. A reranker is a second, slower model that reads the question and one candidate article together and gives them a relevance score, so it can reorder the top results more carefully than distance alone. A standard pipeline retrieves fifty with the embedder and reranks to five. Embedders get a fraction of the attention the generator gets, so here are the models people reach for.

Qwen3-Embedding reported the leading multilingual MTEB score at release (MTEB is a public benchmark suite for embedding models), and it ships in three sizes with the same training recipe. Those three sizes matter more than the headline score, since they let you profile the 0.6B and upgrade only if measurement says the larger one earns its cost.

Three practical points follow that MTEB rank won't tell you. The first is that dimension, the length of each vector, is a storage decision. 4096 floats per chunk against 384 is more than a ten-fold difference in index size and query cost, and several models support truncating the vector, so you can trade a little accuracy for a lot of memory. The second is that MTEB is a public benchmark like any other. Models are tuned against it, and rankings compress at the top and rarely survive contact with a domain corpus, which for us means your own help articles.

Third, rerankers buy more than a bigger embedder. A reranker like bge-reranker-v2-m3 is a cross-encoder, a model that reads the query and the document together, and adding a small one over the top fifty results usually beats upgrading the embedding model, at a fraction of the reindexing cost. Page 11 covers where this sits in a retrieval system, including chunking, which is the other half of the ceiling. We now know every part of the assistant's stack, so the last question is the one finance will ask.

08The cost model

Two arithmetic facts settle most build-versus-buy arguments, and both fit in a paragraph.

One applies to APIs. Pricing is quoted separately for input and output tokens, with output typically several times the price of input. That blended price we saw in the frontier table assumes a fixed ratio, commonly three input tokens to one output token. Your ratio is probably not that, since the assistant sends a question plus several articles in and gets a short answer out. So recompute the cost for your own traffic:

$$\text{cost per request} = T_{\text{in}} \cdot p_{\text{in}} + T_{\text{out}} \cdot p_{\text{out}}$$

With reasoning models, $T_{\text{out}}$ includes the tokens spent thinking, which you pay for and never see.

That last point is the one that surprises finance. A reasoning model at maximum effort can emit ten times the visible output in hidden reasoning tokens. Advertised per-token prices stay the same, and the bill is not.

Another applies to self-hosting, where you pay for the GPU by the hour and the cost per token depends on how many tokens that GPU produces. Comparing options is a throughput calculation:

$$\text{cost per 1M tokens} = \frac{C_{\text{gpu-hour}}}{\text{tokens/sec} \times 3600} \times 10^{6}$$

Tokens per second is the aggregate across all concurrent requests and is different from the single-stream figure.

That distinction is where self-hosting estimates usually go wrong. Single-stream decode (one user, one reply being written) is memory-bandwidth-bound and slow, while aggregate throughput at high concurrency can be twenty times higher on the same card. Quote the wrong one and you're out by an order of magnitude in either direction. Page 31 derives why.

Two more levers beat model choice. Prompt caching removes the prefill cost on a repeated prefix, which is most of the bill for agent loops (and for our assistant, whose long instructions are identical on every request). Routing sends easy requests to a small model and only escalates the hard ones. That routinely cuts spend by more than half without a quality change anyone notices, and it's the job we gave the 0.6B model in section 05.

09Summary

  1. Constraints decide the model before scores do. Ask whether the weights can leave your network, which licence you accept, what latency you can tolerate and how much context you need, and the field shrinks to about three.
  2. Pick the smallest model that passes your own eval. Best on a ranked list and good enough for your task are often different models, and the second is usually much cheaper.
  3. Each benchmark measures one specific thing and fails in a specific way. MMLU-Pro is saturated, GPQA and AIME are small, and LiveCodeBench, HLE and LMArena each cover a gap the others leave.
  4. Four traps distort scores: reasoning effort, the scaffold around the model, contamination and vendor self-reporting. Compare like with like, and prefer third-party harnesses.
  5. Read price and speed next to the index. Across the top six frontier models the index spans six points, while price spans more than six-fold and speed more than three-fold.
  6. In a mixture-of-experts model, total parameters set the memory and active parameters set the speed. Kimi K3 needs the memory of a 2.8T model and decodes at about the cost of a 104B one.
  7. Memory for weights is parameters times bytes per parameter. A 27B model at 4-bit takes about 14 GB before the KV cache, which tells you what fits on your machine.
  8. Small models have narrow jobs, such as drafting, classifying, routing and structured extraction, and a smaller model plus retrieval often beats the largest one that fits.
  9. Read the licence. Open weights aren't open source, revenue thresholds exist, and you should check distillation rights and what your fine-tuned derivative inherits.
  10. Retrieval sets the ceiling for RAG. A small reranker over the top fifty usually beats a bigger embedder, and vector dimension is a storage decision.
  11. Cost per request uses your own token counts, and hidden reasoning tokens count. For self-hosting, use aggregate throughput, then lower the bill with prompt caching and routing.

10Build this

The single highest-value artefact in this whole area is an eval you trust. It takes a day and it outlives every model on this page.

Project The fifty-prompt bake-off ~1 day · any three candidate models

Build the small, ugly, specific eval that decides your model choice, and instrument it so it reports quality, latency and cost together. Public leaderboards pick the candidates, and this eval picks the winner.

  1. Collect fifty real inputs from your own traffic or your own backlog. Avoid synthetic ones. Include the five that currently fail, because those are the ones a model change has to fix.
  2. Write the grading rule before you look at any output. Exact match where you can, a rubric where you cannot, a stronger model as judge only where a human would agree with it.
  3. Run three candidates from different tiers: one frontier API, one large open-weight model, one small model you could host. Record output, wall-clock latency and token counts for every request.
  4. Grade blind by shuffling and stripping the model names before a human looks at anything.
  5. Plot quality against cost per request, with latency as the point size, and leave rank off the plot.
You'll know it worked when the plot has a model in the lower-left that is close enough on quality to the top-right one that you hesitate. That hesitation is the entire value of the exercise: you have found the cheap option that is good enough, and you found it with evidence instead of a hunch.
The stretch that pays for itself. Run the same fifty prompts weekly against your production model and keep the results. When a vendor silently updates a model behind a stable name, your grader catches it in a week rather than a customer catching it in a month. This is the cheapest regression test in the stack, and almost nobody has one.

11What breaks

Model selection fails quietly, and these failures show up after the decision has been made.

  • The leaderboard model does not fit your task. Aggregate indices average over tasks you do not run. A model ranked fourth overall can be first on your workload, and the only way to find out is to measure.
  • The API model changed underneath you. Vendors update models behind stable names. Formatting, tone and refusal behaviour shift without a version bump. Pin dated model identifiers where the vendor offers them.
  • Advertised context is not usable context. A million-token window does not mean a million tokens of reliable reasoning. Test retrieval accuracy at the depth you plan to use.
  • Quantisation changed the model you benchmarked. The 4-bit build you deploy is not the FP16 model whose scores you read. Re-run your eval on the exact artefact you will ship.
  • The licence at scale. A revenue-tiered licence is free until the quarter it stops being free, and by then the model is load-bearing.
  • Hidden reasoning tokens broke the budget. Cost was estimated from visible output length. Reasoning models bill for thinking.
  • The scaffold was the bottleneck. Teams upgrade the model when the retry logic, the tool schemas or the chunking was the problem. Change one thing at a time.

12Pick by use case

Six common shapes, and the shortlist each one implies. Start here, then run section 10.

Customer-facing chat

Time to first token is the product. Output speed matters more than the last two index points, and prompt caching on the system prompt pays for itself immediately.

Shortlist: a fast frontier model, or GLM-5.3-Flash / Qwen3.8-Flash-Next self-hosted.

Coding agent

Long-horizon tool use, where Terminal-Bench and SWE-bench are the relevant signals. It is the workload where the frontier tier still earns its price, and the scaffold is half the score.

Shortlist: top-of-index frontier models; Kimi K3 or GLM-5.3 if weights must stay in-house.

High-volume extraction

Millions of documents to a fixed schema. Nobody is waiting, so latency is irrelevant and cost per million tokens is the only metric that survives.

Shortlist: the smallest model that passes your schema check, batched at high concurrency.

Not a reasoning model at maximum effort.

Regulated or air-gapped

The weights cannot leave, so the choice reduces to the open-weight table, filtered by what your hardware budget can hold and what your licence review will approve.

Shortlist: Apache 2.0 and MIT models first, because the review is short.

On-device

Fixed memory, fixed battery, no network. Quality expectations have to be set by what fits, and the task usually has to be narrowed to match.

Shortlist: Gemma 4 E2B/E4B, Granite 4.2 3B, Qwen3-0.6B for narrow jobs. Page 30 for the sizing.

RAG over your own corpus

The generator is rarely the bottleneck. Retrieval quality is, and that is an embedding, chunking and reranking problem before it is a model-choice problem.

Shortlist: spend the effort on section 07 and a mid-size generator.

13Interview questions

BeginnerWhat is the difference between open weights and open source?

Open weights means the trained parameter file can be downloaded and run locally. Open source, in the established sense, means a licence with no field-of-use restrictions, and under the OSI's AI definition it also implies meaningful disclosure about training data and code. Most models the press calls open source are open weights under a custom licence, which may carry revenue thresholds, attribution requirements or restrictions on training other models from their outputs. The practical question is never which label applies, but whether the specific licence permits the specific deployment you have in mind.

BeginnerIn a mixture-of-experts model, why do total and active parameters both matter?

Total parameters set the memory requirement, because every expert must be resident even though only a few run per token. Active parameters set the compute and memory traffic of a single decode step, so they determine speed and arithmetic cost. A model with 2.8 trillion total and 104 billion active parameters decodes at roughly the cost of a 104-billion dense model but needs the memory footprint of a 2.8-trillion one. That combination is excellent economics on a large served cluster and unusable on a single workstation, which is why quoting only one of the two numbers is misleading.

IntermediateA vendor reports 90% on a benchmark and an independent harness reports 78%. What explains the gap?

Several things, and usually more than one at once. The vendor may run at a higher reasoning-effort setting, which is a different price and latency point on the same weights. The scaffold differs: retry policy, tool definitions, context management and output parsing can move agentic scores by double digits. Prompt formatting and few-shot examples differ. The evaluation harness may parse answers more strictly, counting near-misses as failures. None of these imply dishonesty, but they do mean a vendor number is not directly comparable to a third-party number, and the decision should rest on a run you control.

IntermediateHow would you choose between a frontier API and a self-hosted open-weight model?

Start with the constraints that are not negotiable. If the data cannot leave the network, the decision is already made. Otherwise compare on three axes: quality measured on your own eval rather than a leaderboard, total cost at your projected volume including the engineering time to run a serving stack, and operational risk, which cuts both ways since an API can change under you and a self-hosted model is yours to keep running. Self-hosting tends to win at high, steady volume with a narrow task, because you can use a small model and saturate the hardware. APIs tend to win at low or spiky volume and at the frontier of capability.

IntermediateWhy is a single AIME score a weak signal?

Because the contest has only fifteen problems, so each one is worth about 6.7 percentage points. Sampling noise alone can move a single-run score by more than the gap between two models being compared. Reasoning models are also stochastic, so repeated runs on the same weights disagree. A defensible AIME number is an average over many runs with a reported variance, and comparing two single runs tells you very little. The same argument applies with less force to any small benchmark, including GPQA Diamond.

DeepYour inference bill tripled after switching to a reasoning model, but request volume is flat. Why?

Because reasoning models bill for tokens you never see. The model emits an extended internal chain before the visible answer, and those tokens are charged at the output rate, which is typically several times the input rate. A cost estimate built from visible response length will therefore understate the bill by a large multiple, and the multiple grows with the reasoning-effort setting. The fixes are to lower the effort setting where the task does not need it, to route only hard requests to the reasoning model, and to estimate cost from measured total output tokens rather than from what appears on screen.

DeepHow would you build an evaluation that actually decides a model choice?

Take fifty real inputs from production traffic, deliberately including current failures, and fix the grading rule before seeing any output. Use exact match or a programmatic check wherever the task permits it, a written rubric where it does not, and a model judge only where a human would agree with the judge. Run every candidate through the identical scaffold, record latency and token counts alongside quality, and grade blind with model names stripped. Then plot quality against cost rather than ranking, because the decision is a trade, not an ordering. Re-run it on a schedule so that a silent vendor-side model update is caught by your harness rather than by a customer.

DeepWhat does a one-million-token context window actually buy you?

Less than the number suggests. Retrieval of a single fact from deep in the window is now largely solved, but reasoning that requires combining many facts spread across the window degrades well before the advertised limit. Long contexts also cost real resources: prefill time grows with prompt length and directly sets time to first token, and the KV cache grows linearly with the context, so concurrency falls as context rises. In practice a retrieval step that puts five relevant pages in a short context usually beats putting five hundred pages in a long one, and it is cheaper on every axis. Test at the depth you intend to use before designing around the headline number.
