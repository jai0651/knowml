AI in Industry: What It Solves

You will build a four-question filter for claims about AI in science and medicine, then apply it to seven cases, from protein folding to mammography. By the end you will see why the wins share one pattern.

Every few months a headline announces that AI has solved something in science or medicine, and from the headline alone it's hard to tell which of these will hold up. A few have held up better than anyone expected. A protein-structure model won a Nobel Prize. AI weather forecasting is running operationally at Europe's forecasting centre. A randomised trial of 105,934 women found that AI-supported mammography catches more cancers, with 44% less radiologist reading time.

Other famous results are weaker than their headlines, and a few of the best-sounding ones have very little independent checking behind them. So before looking at any of them we'll build a short filter of four questions (section 01), and then run seven cases through it one after another: protein structures, weather, materials, chip design, fusion plasma control, mammography and algorithm discovery. Where the answer is unflattering, we'll say so.

Doing that turns up a pattern. The cases that worked are the ones where the space of possible answers is enormous but checking any single answer is cheap. The ones that stalled are the ones where nothing cheap exists to check against. By section 09 we'll have a picture that places every case on those two properties, and you should then be able to guess where a new claim will land, probably before you've read its details.

Everything here was peer-reviewed, deployed or tested in a randomised trial, apart from a few results that didn't survive scrutiny. It's a snapshot as of 9 September 2026. The page assumes no background, so each term is explained where it first appears.

01How to read a claim

Suppose a headline says an AI system has transformed some field. What would you want to know before believing it? Four questions take about a minute to ask, and each one catches a different way a result can be weaker than it sounds.

The first is whether the result has been through peer review or is only a blog post or press release. In peer review, independent experts with no stake in the result read the paper critically before a journal publishes it. Both kinds of source can be right, but only one has had someone hostile read it carefully.

The second is whether the result was deployed or only demonstrated. A model that beats a baseline on a held-out set, meaning data it never saw during training, has been demonstrated. A model running in production on live inputs that nobody curated has been deployed. These are different achievements, and the gap between them is where most projects die.

Third, compared against what? A baseline is the method a new result is measured against. The relevant one is the best existing method operated competently, and a weak version of it, or no method at all, makes almost anything look good.

The fourth question is whether anyone independent has reproduced the result. Reproduction is where several of the results later on this page came apart, so it's worth asking even when the first three answers look fine. A result that's perhaps right but unreplicated deserves a lower confidence than one that's been rebuilt elsewhere.

To see how much these questions matter, look at medical devices. A 2026 review examined 1,357 AI-enabled medical devices cleared by the FDA, the US regulator. Of those, 34 were linked to a registered prospective trial, meaning a trial whose plan was filed before the results existed. Twelve had a peer-reviewed publication. Three had been evaluated against patient-centred outcomes such as mortality, morbidity or readmission. That's 3 out of 1,357, roughly 0.2%.

Why isn't "FDA-cleared" evidence that a device helps?

Because clearance answers a different question. For the common pathway, it means the device was shown to be substantially equivalent to something already on the market, and it says nothing on whether patients do better for using it. So read "FDA-cleared" as "legally sellable", and don't read it as "proven to help patients". Keep the figure of three in 1,357 in mind, because we'll meet the rare device that did get tested properly in section 07.

With the filter ready, we need a case to run it on. The cleanest one comes from biology, where a fifty-year-old problem got an answer that was checked against experiment.

02Structural biology: the strongest case there is

A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds into a specific three-dimensional shape, and the shape determines what the protein does. Measuring that shape in a lab, by X-ray crystallography or cryo-electron microscopy, historically took months to years per protein. Predicting it from the amino-acid sequence alone was a fifty-year open problem, with a biennial competition called CASP that tracked how badly everyone was doing.

AlphaFold 2 ended that in 2020. At CASP14 it reached accuracy comparable to experimental structures for most targets, and the consequences were unusually concrete. Coverage went from thousands of proteins to nearly all of them: the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database now holds predicted structures for over 200 million protein sequences, almost every protein known to science, released openly. Adoption followed quickly, with more than two million researchers across around 190 countries having used it. And in 2024 the work won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry, shared by Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for AlphaFold and David Baker for computational protein design.

Why was this problem winnable?

Look at what the researchers had to work with. Fifty years of experimentally determined structures in the Protein Data Bank gave a large, high-quality training set that was grounded in physical measurement. CASP gave a blind benchmark, scored independently, that nobody could game, because the answers were withheld until predictions were in. And the target was a single well-posed output, a set of coordinates. Labelled data, an honest scorer and one clear output: when a scientific problem has that shape, expect machine learning to make serious progress, and be sceptical of anyone promising the same without those three things.

What AlphaFold didn't do is routinely oversold, so it's worth being a bit more exact. It predicts a structure, typically one conformation, while real proteins move between states and their function often depends on that motion. Predictions for intrinsically disordered regions, large allosteric transitions and ligand binding remain much weaker. AlphaFold 3 extended coverage to complexes, nucleic acids and ligands. Drug discovery, though, needs dynamics and binding affinity, and a static structure doesn't give you either.

Protein folding passes the filter because it was scored against known answers. Can a model also earn trust when it's scored on something that hasn't happened yet? Weather forecasting is the obvious test.

03Weather: the strongest case for deployment

Numerical weather prediction integrates the equations of atmospheric physics on a supercomputer, stepping the atmosphere forward in time. It's one of computing's great successes and it's expensive: a global forecast takes hours on a dedicated cluster drawing megawatts. Learned models changed the economics first, and then the accuracy.

GraphCast (Science, 2023) was a graph neural network, a network that works on points joined by connections, here points spread over the globe (chapter 18 covers them). It beat the operational deterministic baseline, which produces a single forecast, on most variables and lead times, and it ran in minutes. GenCast (Nature, December 2024) went further and made probabilistic forecasts, meaning many plausible futures with probabilities attached. It outperformed Europe's leading ensemble system, a set of forecasts run from slightly different starting conditions, on 97.2% of 1,320 evaluation targets. That's roughly 1,283 of them. It produced a 15-day global forecast in about 8 minutes on a single TPU, an accelerator chip built for machine learning.

Is that a demo, or is it in use? It's in use. ECMWF, Europe's forecasting centre, put its own AI Forecasting System, AIFS, into operations on 25 February 2025, running alongside the physics-based system, with reported gains of up to 20% on measures including tropical cyclone tracks.

Run the four questions on it. The work is peer-reviewed. It's deployed at the institution whose forecasts feed European national weather services. It's measured against the best existing system, and that institution ran it independently, not only the model's authors. It passes all four.

There's one caveat, and it concerns the training data. These models learn from reanalysis, decades of past weather records re-processed into a consistent gridded history, so their competence is anchored in the climate that produced that data. How they behave on unprecedented events is an active research question, and unprecedented events are where forecasting matters most.

Two cases have now passed, and both had a large body of honest scoring behind them. What happens when the work is real but the scoring is weaker, and the headline gets ahead of it?

04Materials: where the headline outran the result

In November 2023 DeepMind published GNoME in Nature, reporting 2.2 million predicted crystal structures, of which around 380,000 were predicted stable. The announcement described this as discovering millions of new materials, and compared the haul to centuries of accumulated human knowledge.

Materials scientists then looked at the output. Anthony Cheetham and Ram Seshadri of UC Santa Barbara sampled the released structures and applied a three-part test: is each proposed compound credible, useful and novel? They found no strikingly novel compounds in the sample, and characterised much of the set as combinatorial variations and orderings of known compositions.

Why isn't a list of candidates the same as a discovery?

Both things are true at once, so it helps to separate them. GNoME predicts stability at a throughput no human workflow could match, and that's a real contribution to a real bottleneck. But a list of 380,000 plausible crystals isn't 380,000 discoveries, because a discovery needs somebody who wants the material, can make it, and finds that it does something new. None of that is checked by the prediction. So when a result is a list of candidates, the interesting number is the hit rate after somebody tries to use it, and the length of the list tells you little.

The scope is narrow too. The predictions cover inorganic crystalline compounds. Polymers, glasses, metal-organic frameworks, composites and heterostructures, where a great deal of applied materials work happens, weren't addressed.

The materials case fails on novelty and independent checking, but the underlying work is sound. The next case is nearly the reverse: nobody doubts the organisation uses it, but outsiders can't confirm how well it works.

05Chip design: real deployment, contested benchmark

Placing blocks on a chip die, a task called floorplanning, is a combinatorial optimisation problem that human engineers spend weeks on. DeepMind published a reinforcement-learning approach in Nature in 2021, later branded AlphaChip. In reinforcement learning a model learns by trying actions and receiving a score for how things turned out, and chapter 15 explains it in full. DeepMind reports using the method in the design of multiple generations of Google's TPUs.

Both halves of the story matter here. After criticism, Nature completed a post-publication review in 2024 and published an Addendum in September 2024 supporting the original work, so the journal investigated and upheld it. On the other side, independent verification remains limited. Critics including Rice University's Moshe Vardi have said plainly that the community has not been able to verify the claims, and competing benchmark studies reached less favourable conclusions.

A fair reading is probably that the method is in production at the organisation that built it, and that's meaningful evidence, while the published comparisons aren't strong enough to tell you how it would perform in your own design flow. This situation is common and under-acknowledged for industrial AI results, where the deployment is proprietary and the public benchmark is only a proxy for the real job.

Floorplanning is a puzzle that lives entirely in software. What about reinforcement learning that has to steer physical hardware, where a bad decision can damage the machine?

06Physical control: fusion plasma

A tokamak is a doughnut-shaped fusion reactor that confines plasma at over a hundred million degrees using magnetic fields, and the fields are adjusted thousands of times a second. Designing those controllers has traditionally meant hand-built control laws for each plasma shape.

DeepMind and EPFL published work in Nature in 2022 in which a reinforcement-learning policy commanded the full set of control coils on the TCV tokamak in Lausanne. It held conventional elongated shapes and advanced configurations including negative triangularity and a "snowflake", and it sustained two separate plasmas in the vessel at once.

What makes it notable is less the physics score than how the policy got there. It was trained in simulation and then ran on the real machine. Crossing that sim-to-real gap, from a simulated world to a physical one where the two never match exactly, is one of the hardest things reinforcement learning has been asked to do, and it's harder still on a device where a control failure damages hardware. Chapter 21 covers why that transfer is so difficult.

So far every case has been about science or engineering, where a wrong answer costs time or money. Medicine is where a wrong answer costs a patient, and the question there is what it looks like when an AI tool is tested the way a drug would be.

07Medicine: the one that reached patients properly

Thousands of medical AI products exist, and the 1,357 devices from section 01 are only the cleared ones. Very few have been tested the way a drug would be. The MASAI trial in Sweden is one that was. It randomised 105,934 women, which means a process much like a coin flip assigned each woman to either AI-supported mammography screening or standard double reading by two radiologists. Chance assignment is what lets you attribute any difference in results to the AI, since nothing else systematically differs between the groups. It's a randomised, controlled, population-based screening trial, and the results have been published across The Lancet family of journals.

To read the results, you need two terms. An interval cancer is one that shows up between screening rounds, which means the screening missed it. Specificity is how well the programme leaves healthy women alone, so a higher figure means fewer women called back for a scare that turns out to be nothing.

Read the interval cancer row first, because it matters most. It fell from 1.76 to 1.55 per 1,000, roughly 12% lower, and fewer interval cancers means the programme is catching disease earlier, and catching disease earlier is the whole purpose of screening. Specificity rose by 6.7 percentage points, from 73.8% to 80.5%. The workload figure comes from an earlier interim safety analysis, so it's a separate result from the other two rows.

Set this against the number from section 01. Out of 1,357 cleared AI medical devices, three had been evaluated on patient outcomes. MASAI shows what the standard could look like, and it gives no evidence that the field in general meets it.

Our last category goes back to the easy end of checking, and it has one of the neatest results of the lot.

08Algorithms: AI improving the tools it runs on

Matrix multiplication sits underneath almost all of machine learning, so a faster way to do it matters everywhere. Multiplying two 4×4 matrices the schoolbook way takes 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 scalar multiplications, where a scalar multiplication is one multiplication of two plain numbers. Finding a scheme that needs fewer is a search problem, and a candidate scheme can be checked by arithmetic, mechanically and exactly.

AlphaTensor (Nature, 2022) found faster matrix multiplication algorithms by treating algorithm discovery as a game. FunSearch (Nature, 2024) used a language model inside an evolutionary loop to improve a bound on the cap set problem, a genuine open question in combinatorics. AlphaEvolve (2025) found a scheme multiplying two 4×4 matrices in 48 scalar multiplications, improving on the 49 that Strassen's algorithm had held since 1969.

Fifty-six years is a long time for a bound to stand in a problem this heavily studied, and it seems fair to call the result surprising. This category works for the reason the mathematics results in chapter 36 work: any wrong candidate is caught immediately and for free, so the model can be wrong most of the time without costing anyone anything.

That sentence is the beginning of a general rule, and we now have enough cases to state it properly.

09The pattern worth taking away

Go back through the seven cases and sort them by how well they worked. Two properties turn out to predict the outcome better than anything about the models. One is how big the space of possible answers is. The other is how much it costs to check a single answer. Here's every result from this page placed on those two axes.

Everything on the left, where checking is cheap, shipped. Protein structures were scored against known structures, weather forecasts were graded by tomorrow, matrix multiplication schemes were checked by arithmetic, and Lean proofs (chapter 36) were checked by a compiler. Everything on the right produced a list that somebody still has to test. A crystal has to be synthesised, a drug has to go through a multi-year trial, and for the deepest open problems in mathematics nobody knows how to check that a proposed idea is the right one. Mammography sits on the right as well, because only a randomised trial settles it.

Why does a cheap check matter so much?

Because it lets the model be unreliable. If a wrong answer costs nothing to discard, a generator that's right one time in a thousand is still useful, since the filter removes the other 999 for free. If checking takes a lab or a trial, every wrong answer costs real money and time, and prediction throughput stops being the bottleneck. The vertical axis matters separately. Chip floorplanning has a cheap check but a narrower search space, so it's useful without being transformative.

This gives you a practical test for any AI-for-science claim: find the verifier first. Ask what checks the model's output, how much that check costs and how honest it is. If nothing does, the result is a list of hypotheses, which has value but isn't a discovery. Most disappointment in this field is a verifier problem wearing a modelling costume, and it follows that building a cheap verifier for your field can be worth more than building a better model for it.

10What breaks

If the pattern is right, then the failures should mostly be about evidence and not about models. They mostly are, and they come in a handful of recurring forms, perhaps seven in all.

The first group is reading evidence too generously. Clearance gets mistaken for proof: "FDA-cleared" means sellable, and three out of 1,357 cleared devices had been checked against patient outcomes. Candidate lists get counted as discoveries, so 380,000 predicted stable crystals is a starting point, and the number that matters is how many were made and turned out useful. And the benchmark gets mistaken for the job. Chip floorplanning scores on public benchmarks tell you little about a proprietary production flow, in either direction.

The second group is models meeting the real world. Weather models learn from historical reanalysis, and the events people care about most are the ones with the least precedent, so distribution shift hits hardest exactly where it hurts. A single predicted protein structure omits the dynamics that determine binding, and binding is most of what drug discovery needs. Then there's the demo-to-deployment gap, where projects die. Weather succeeded partly because forecasting centres already had the ingestion, verification and operational discipline to absorb a new model, and most fields don't.

One last failure is about how the stories get told. Results built on decades of human technique get reported as the machine's achievement. That's unfair, and it probably misleads people about how the next result will happen.

12Summary

  1. Four questions filter most claims. Was it peer-reviewed, was it deployed or only demonstrated, was it compared against the best existing method, and did anyone independent reproduce it?
  2. "FDA-cleared" means sellable. Of 1,357 cleared AI devices in a 2026 review, 34 were linked to a registered prospective trial, 12 had a peer-reviewed publication and 3 were evaluated on patient outcomes.
  3. Protein structure is the strongest scientific case. AlphaFold had labelled data, a blind benchmark and a single clear output, and it earned a share of the 2024 Chemistry Nobel. It still predicts one conformation and is weak on dynamics and binding.
  4. Weather is the strongest case for deployment. GenCast beat the leading ensemble on 97.2% of 1,320 targets in about 8 minutes on one TPU, and ECMWF put AIFS into operations on 25 February 2025.
  5. GNoME shows a headline outrunning a result. 380,000 predicted stable crystals is a candidate list, and independent reviewers found no strikingly novel compounds in their sample.
  6. Chip floorplanning is deployed but contested. Nature upheld the work in 2024, while independent verification remains limited.
  7. Fusion control crossed the sim-to-real gap. A policy trained in simulation held several plasma shapes on the real TCV tokamak.
  8. MASAI is what proper medical evidence looks like. In 105,934 women, AI-supported screening lowered the interval cancer rate from 1.76 to 1.55 per 1,000 and raised specificity from 73.8% to 80.5%.
  9. Algorithm discovery works because checking is exact. AlphaEvolve's 48-multiplication scheme for 4×4 matrices beat a 1969 record of 49.
  10. AI wins where the search space is huge and verification is cheap. Where checking needs a lab, a trial or an expert, the output is a list of hypotheses.
  11. Find the verifier first. When you assess any new claim, ask what checks the output, what that check costs and how honest it is.

13Interview questions

BeginnerWhat did AlphaFold change, and what did it not?

It made predicting a protein's three-dimensional structure from its amino-acid sequence accurate enough to substitute for experiment in many cases, collapsing a task that took months of crystallography into minutes of compute. The AlphaFold database now covers over 200 million sequences openly, it has been used by more than two million researchers, and the work shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. What it did not do is solve protein behaviour. It typically predicts a single conformation, while real proteins move between states, and predictions remain much weaker for disordered regions, large allosteric changes and ligand binding. Drug discovery needs dynamics and binding affinity, which a static structure does not provide.

BeginnerWhy is "FDA-cleared" weak evidence that a medical AI tool helps patients?

Because clearance under the common pathway establishes substantial equivalence to a device already on the market, not clinical benefit. A 2026 review of 1,357 cleared AI-enabled devices found 34 linked to a registered prospective trial, 12 with a peer-reviewed publication, and only three evaluated against patient-centred outcomes such as mortality, morbidity or readmission. So clearance tells you a product may legally be sold, and says almost nothing about whether using it improves outcomes. The MASAI mammography trial, which randomised over 105,000 women and reported both a lower interval cancer rate and higher specificity, shows what the stronger standard looks like and how rarely it is met.

IntermediateWhy did AI weather forecasting succeed in operational deployment when many AI-for-science projects stall?

Because every prerequisite happened to be in place. There is a large, uniform, high-quality training corpus in decades of reanalysis data. There is a free and completely honest verifier: tomorrow arrives and you score the forecast against it. The baseline is well defined and rigorously tracked, so improvements are measurable rather than arguable. And forecasting centres already had the operational machinery to ingest, verify and serve a new model, which is usually the part that kills a project. GenCast beat the leading ensemble on 97.2% of 1,320 targets while running in about eight minutes on a single accelerator, and ECMWF put its own AI system into operations in February 2025. The scarce ingredient in most other fields is the cheap honest verifier, not the model.

IntermediateWhat was the substance of the criticism of the GNoME materials results?

That the headline conflated predicted candidates with discoveries. DeepMind reported 2.2 million predicted crystal structures with around 380,000 predicted stable, framed as discovering millions of new materials. Cheetham and Seshadri sampled the release and applied a three-part test of credibility, usefulness and novelty, reporting that they found no strikingly novel compounds in their sample and that much of the set consisted of combinatorial variants and orderings of known compositions. They also noted the scope was inorganic crystalline compounds, excluding polymers, glasses, metal-organic frameworks and composites. The defensible version of the result is that stability prediction at very high throughput is genuinely useful; the indefensible version is counting list length as discoveries.

IntermediateWhy is the fusion plasma control result significant beyond fusion?

Because it crossed the sim-to-real gap on a system where failure is expensive. A reinforcement-learning policy was trained against a tokamak simulator and then commanded the real control coils on the TCV device, holding conventional and advanced plasma configurations including negative triangularity and a snowflake, and sustaining two plasmas at once. Most reinforcement learning successes are in simulation or in games, where the environment and the deployment target are identical. Here the policy had to survive the mismatch between a model of the physics and the actual machine, under hard real-time constraints, on hardware that damage matters to. That transfer problem is the central obstacle to reinforcement learning in the physical world, which is why the result generalises well beyond the application.

DeepYou are asked to assess a new AI-for-science claim. What framework would you use?

Find the verifier first: what checks the model's output, how expensive is that check, and is it independent of the model's authors. If verification is cheap and honest, as with a forecast scored against tomorrow's weather or an algorithm checked by arithmetic, then unreliable generation is fine because errors are filtered for free, and the result is likely to hold up. If verification requires synthesis, a clinical trial or expert judgement, the model has produced a hypothesis list and the meaningful number is the downstream hit rate, which is usually unreported. Then apply the standard four checks: peer-reviewed rather than announced, deployed rather than demonstrated, compared against the best existing method competently operated, and independently reproduced. Finally ask what the claim would look like if it were false, and whether the evidence presented would distinguish those cases.

DeepWhich scientific problems should you expect machine learning to make progress on next?

The ones whose structure resembles the successes rather than the disappointments. Look for three things: a large body of consistent, well-measured historical data; a well-posed output the model can be asked for; and a verification signal that is cheap, fast and not gameable. Protein folding had all three, with the Protein Data Bank, coordinates as output, and CASP as a blind benchmark. Weather had all three, with reanalysis archives, gridded forecasts, and tomorrow as the judge. Conversely, expect slow progress where the verifier is a wet-lab experiment, a multi-year trial or a human expert's opinion, because throughput on hypotheses stops being the constraint. The corollary is that building a cheap verifier for a field is often a higher-leverage contribution than building a better model for it.
